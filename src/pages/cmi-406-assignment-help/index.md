## What CMI Unit 406 Covers

Unit 406 addresses organisational culture and business ethics as connected management concerns. The learning outcomes require you to analyse how organisational culture affects management behaviour and team performance, evaluate the manager's role in sustaining or changing culture, and analyse the ethical responsibilities of management within an organisational context. Culture and ethics are presented as interconnected: the organisation's ethical behaviour is substantially a function of its culture — the taken-for-granted assumptions that guide decision-making when no policy specifically applies.

## Handy's Culture Types — Analyse Depth

Charles Handy's four culture types (1985, *Gods of Management*, Pan Books) classify organisational cultures by their power structure, communication style, and core values:

**Power Culture (Zeus — the spider's web)**: power radiates from a central figure. Decisions are fast because authority is concentrated. Culture depends heavily on the central figure's values and judgment. Common in small entrepreneurial organisations and family businesses. Risk: highly vulnerable to poor judgment at the centre; succession is fragile; rules and procedures are weak because the central figure can override them.

**Role Culture (Apollo — the Greek temple)**: power derives from position and formal authority. Rules, procedures, and defined roles structure behaviour. Highly predictable and stable. Common in large bureaucracies, public sector organisations, and regulated industries. Risk: slow to respond to change; values process compliance over outcomes; can produce rule-following at the expense of responsiveness when the situation does not match the procedure.

**Task Culture (Athena — the net)**: power derives from expertise and task competence. Teams are formed and reformed around specific problems. Highly adaptive and creative. Common in consulting, project-based organisations, and innovation environments. Risk: difficult to scale; managers have limited formal authority and must influence through expertise; struggles with routine operational tasks that require stability rather than adaptation.

**Person Culture (Dionysus — the cluster)**: power derives from the individual specialist. The organisation exists to serve the professionals within it. Common in professional partnerships — law firms, medical practices, academic institutions. Risk: difficult to manage; specialists may resist organisational direction; coordination depends on professional norms rather than managerial authority.

At Level 4 Analyse depth: analyse how the dominant culture type in a specific organisation shapes management behaviour. A manager operating with a Task Culture approach (forming flexible expert teams, distributing authority by competence) within a Role Culture organisation will encounter structural resistance — the organisation's procedures, job descriptions, and authority structures are designed for role-based rather than task-based power distribution. The analytical question is not "which culture is best?" but "how does the current culture type constrain or enable the specific management approach?"

At Evaluate depth: evaluate whether the current culture type is aligned with the organisation's strategic context. A Role Culture organisation facing rapid market change needs to develop Task Culture capacity — but cannot do so without changing the underlying power distribution and tolerance for ambiguity that role culture depends on.

## Schein's Three Levels of Culture — Analyse Depth

Edgar Schein's model (1985, *Organizational Culture and Leadership*, Jossey-Bass) describes organisational culture as operating at three levels of depth:

**Level 1 — Artefacts**: visible cultural expressions — physical environment, dress code, the language used in meetings, rituals, ceremonies, and formal processes. Artefacts are observable but their cultural meaning cannot be reliably inferred from surface observation alone. An open-plan office (artefact) may signal collaboration culture or may reflect cost reduction with no cultural intent.

**Level 2 — Espoused Values**: the stated values, mission statements, codes of conduct, and publicly articulated principles that the organisation claims to hold. These are what the organisation says it values. Espoused values may or may not align with actual behaviour — "We value our people" is an espoused value; the organisation's actual redundancy practice, workload management, and investment in development reveals whether the espoused value is operationalised.

**Level 3 — Basic Underlying Assumptions**: the taken-for-granted beliefs, unconscious assumptions, and automatic responses that are never questioned because they are experienced as self-evidently true. These drive behaviour automatically, without conscious decision-making. Examples: "The organisation cannot be wrong about strategic decisions — the senior leadership always knows best"; "Risk-taking is punished here, regardless of outcome"; "Managers are here to maintain control, not to develop capability." Basic assumptions are the most deeply embedded level of culture and the most resistant to change.

At Level 4 Analyse depth: the connection between Schein's three levels is the analytical core. Where espoused values and basic assumptions diverge, the basic assumptions will govern actual behaviour — not the espoused values. An organisation that espouses psychological safety (Level 2) but whose basic assumption (Level 3) is that challenge of senior authority produces career risk will produce a culture where psychological safety language appears in documents but silence operates in practice. The artefacts — meeting formats, 360-degree feedback tools, speaking-up policies — exist at Level 1 without the Level 3 change that would make them effective.

## Carroll's CSR Pyramid — Analyse Depth

Archie Carroll's Pyramid of Corporate Social Responsibility (1991) identifies four categories of organisational responsibility, stacked from foundational to aspirational:

**Economic Responsibility** (foundational): be profitable. Organisations cannot fulfil other responsibilities without the economic foundation to do so.
**Legal Responsibility**: obey the law. The legal minimum of behaviour.
**Ethical Responsibility**: do what is right, fair, and just beyond what the law requires.
**Philanthropic Responsibility** (aspirational): contribute to the community and improve quality of life beyond what is ethically required.

At Level 4 Analyse depth: analyse what the pyramid structure implies about the relationship between the four responsibilities. Carroll argues that these responsibilities exist simultaneously — a genuinely responsible organisation addresses all four at once, not sequentially. However, the pyramid's hierarchy is significant: organisations under severe economic pressure may retreat from ethical and philanthropic responsibilities to focus on economic survival and legal compliance. Analysing where an organisation is investing its CSR attention — and what that reveals about its actual values versus espoused values — connects Carroll's pyramid back to Schein's model.

The ethical responsibility tier is the analytically richest for management application: it covers behaviour that is legal but questionable — practices that exploit regulatory gaps, communication that is technically accurate but misleading, or workplace practices that comply with employment law while generating outcomes that are unfair or harmful.

## Pass / Merit / Distinction

**Pass:** Handy's four culture types are described and one is applied to a workplace. Schein's three levels are described with examples. Carroll's pyramid is described. Workplace ethics example is included.

**Merit:** Handy's culture type is analysed — how the culture type shapes management behaviour, not just what it looks like. Schein's model is applied to the gap between espoused values and basic assumptions in the specific organisation. Carroll's pyramid is applied to analyse the organisation's ethical standing.

**Distinction — worked example:** "Schein's basic assumptions in my organisation — that operational continuity takes precedence over individual wellbeing and that raising concerns is career-limiting — operate as cultural Level 3 constraints that undermine the espoused Level 2 values of psychological safety and people-first culture. The artefacts (Level 1) of a staff survey, a speaking-up policy, and a Freedom to Speak Up Guardian signal a Task Culture orientation (Handy) where expertise and voice are valued. But the basic assumptions produce a Role Culture behavioural reality — staff follow process without challenge because the basic assumption is that challenge is unsafe. Carroll's pyramid analysis places this organisation in a position where it meets Legal and most Economic responsibilities, aspires to Ethical responsibility in its espoused values, but fails to fulfil it in its actual practice — because the basic cultural assumption that speaking up is career-limiting prevents the ethical responsibility from being operationalised. The implication for management: changing the artefacts (surveys, policies) without addressing the basic assumption that drives the silence will not shift ethical behaviour."

## Common Questions About CMI Unit 406

**What is the difference between organisational culture and organisational climate?**
Organisational culture (Schein) refers to the deep, embedded values and assumptions that shape the organisation's basic character — it is relatively stable and slow to change. Organisational climate refers to the shared perceptions of working conditions and managerial practices at a given point in time — it can change relatively quickly in response to leadership behaviour, events, or structural changes. Culture is the "who we are"; climate is "how it feels to work here right now." At Level 4, both concepts may be relevant: culture provides the deep explanation for observed behaviour; climate provides the more immediate, adjustable dimension that management can influence.

**Is Handy's model outdated — are there more modern culture frameworks I should use?**
Handy (1985) remains the standard framework for Unit 406 and is the framework specifically covered in CMI Level 4 materials. More recent culture frameworks (such as Cameron and Quinn's Competing Values Framework, or Denison's Organizational Culture Model) offer alternative analytical lenses, but using them in Unit 406 requires connecting them to the CMI curriculum frameworks. If your brief specifies Handy, use Handy. If you wish to add a second framework for comparative analysis, Cameron and Quinn is the most compatible modern alternative — cite as: Cameron, K.S. and Quinn, R.E. (2011) *Diagnosing and Changing Organizational Culture*. 3rd edn. Jossey-Bass.

**How do I write about my organisation's culture without being critical of my employer?**
Unit 406 requires analysis of organisational culture, which includes identifying gaps between espoused values and actual practice (Schein). This is analytical work, not criticism. Frame the analysis professionally: "The organisation's espoused values emphasise collaboration (Level 2), and meeting room availability and cross-functional project structures support this (Level 1). However, performance management frameworks that reward individual output rather than team contribution reflect a basic assumption (Level 3) that individual accountability is the primary value — creating a tension between the espoused collaborative values and the performance structures that actually shape behaviour." This is analysis, not complaint. Anonymise if needed.

**Carroll's pyramid — is CSR a Level 4 unit topic or more of a Level 5/7 topic?**
Carroll's pyramid is explicitly introduced at Level 4 in Unit 406 as a framework for understanding business ethics at the management level. Level 5 and Level 7 engage with CSR at greater critical depth — Level 5 evaluates the tension between stakeholder and shareholder models; Level 7 critically analyses Carroll vs Freeman vs Elkington. At Level 4, Carroll's pyramid is applied to analyse the organisation's ethical responsibilities and evaluate where it sits on the pyramid — this is appropriate analytical depth for Level 4 and does not need to engage with the Level 7 academic debate about CSR theory.

**Should Unit 406 include examples from my organisation or general examples?**
At Level 4, the strongest essays apply frameworks to a specific, real organisational context — typically the student's own organisation or one they know well. Generic examples ("many organisations have role cultures") do not demonstrate analytical depth. Specific examples ("the organisation's emphasis on procedure-following and approval hierarchies for even minor spending decisions reflects Role Culture artefacts, suggesting Apollo power distribution") demonstrate that the student can apply the framework as a diagnostic tool. Anonymise as needed, but maintain specificity.
