## What CMI Unit 307 Covers

Unit 307 addresses the development dimension of first-line management, how managers identify what team members need to learn, how individuals prefer to learn, and how to plan and support development through formal and informal means. The learning outcomes require you to describe methods for identifying development needs, explain approaches to supporting individual and team development, and describe how to evaluate the effectiveness of development activities. Command verbs are Describe and Explain.

## Training Needs Analysis

A training needs analysis (TNA) is the systematic process of identifying the gap between the knowledge, skills, and abilities a team member currently has and those required to perform their role effectively or develop into a future role. At first-line manager level, TNA is conducted through a combination of: observation of day-to-day performance; one-to-one supervision conversations; performance review discussions; review of quality data and error rates; and team-level skills gap mapping (identifying which team members hold which capabilities and where coverage gaps exist).

The three-level TNA model distinguishes: **Organisational level** (what does the organisation as a whole need its workforce to be able to do, driven by strategy, regulatory requirements, and change programmes?); **Role level** (what knowledge, skills, and behaviours does the specific role require, as set out in job descriptions, competency frameworks, or professional standards?); and **Individual level** (what does this specific team member currently do well, and where is there a gap between their current capability and the role requirement?).

At Level 3, TNA is explained primarily at the individual and role level, the first-line manager's sphere of direct influence. A development need is only a training need if: a performance gap exists; the gap is caused by a knowledge or skill deficit (not by motivation, resources, or clarity of expectation); and development activity will close the gap. A manager who sends a team member on a training course to address a performance problem caused by unclear expectations is not conducting TNA, they are responding to a symptom rather than diagnosing the cause.

## Learning Styles — Honey and Mumford

Peter Honey and Alan Mumford's learning styles model (1982, building on Kolb's earlier experiential learning cycle) identifies four learning style preferences: Activist, Reflector, Theorist, and Pragmatist.

**Activist**: learns best from doing, new experiences, challenges, and hands-on activity. Activists prefer practical tasks, role play, and group activities over lectures or written materials. **Reflector**: learns best from observation and reflection, reviewing what happened before drawing conclusions. Reflectors prefer shadowing, observation, and structured reflection opportunities. **Theorist**: learns best from models, concepts, and frameworks, understanding the theory before applying it. Theorists prefer structured courses, reading materials, and opportunities to connect practice to theory. **Pragmatist**: learns best from applying learning to real problems, practical relevance is essential. Pragmatists prefer action learning, coaching, and project-based learning with clear real-world application.

At Describe depth: describe each style and give a workplace example of a development activity suited to each. At Explain depth: explain why matching development activity to learning style preference improves development effectiveness, a Reflector sent on an intensive Activist-oriented workshop (high activity, rapid pace, minimal reflection time) will not learn effectively even if the content is relevant. A Theorist given only on-the-job task repetition without access to the theoretical framework behind the task will struggle to generalise their learning to new situations.

The limitation at Distinction depth: Honey and Mumford's model suggests individuals have a dominant learning style preference, but research evidence on the stability and predictive validity of learning styles is contested. At Level 3, noting that people often benefit from multiple learning methods regardless of style preference, and that the 70:20:10 model (70% experience, 20% feedback, 10% formal training) reflects this broader view, demonstrates analytical depth beyond simple application of the styles model.

## The 70:20:10 Development Model

The 70:20:10 model (Lombardo and Eichinger, 1996) proposes that effective development in the workplace draws approximately 70% from on-the-job experience, 20% from learning through feedback and relationships (coaching, mentoring, peer learning), and 10% from formal training events (courses, workshops, e-learning). The proportions are indicative rather than precise, but the model's key insight is that most learning happens through experience and feedback, not through formal training.

For first-line managers, the 70:20:10 model has practical implications: most of the development investment available to a team leader involves how work is structured, supervised, and reflected upon, not how many training courses are booked. A first-line manager who provides regular coaching conversations, creates stretch assignment opportunities, ensures good-quality feedback after tasks, and facilitates peer learning within the team is delivering 70% and 20% development far more effectively than one who books team members onto external courses but provides no feedback or development conversation.

## Formal and Informal Development Methods

Development methods at first-line management level include both formal (planned, structured, often external) and informal (embedded in daily work) approaches.

**Formal methods**: training courses (face-to-face and e-learning); professional qualifications; induction programmes; structured coaching (with an external or internal coach); mentoring programmes; job rotation schemes; secondments. **Informal methods**: on-the-job coaching by the line manager; stretch assignments (tasks slightly beyond current capability); peer learning and knowledge sharing within the team; reflection after critical incidents; shadowing more experienced colleagues; reading and self-directed learning.

At Level 3, the key management decision is matching the development method to the learning need, the learner's style preference, and the resources available. A team member who needs to develop a specific technical skill may benefit from a formal training course. A team member who needs to develop broader judgement and problem-solving capability benefits more from stretch assignments and coaching conversations, formal training cannot replicate the contextual learning that comes from doing.

## Evaluating Development Effectiveness

Kirkpatrick's four-level evaluation model (1959, *Journal of American Society of Training Directors*) provides the framework for assessing whether development activity has been effective.

**Level 1, Reaction**: did the learner enjoy and engage with the activity? (Post-training feedback forms, satisfaction surveys.) **Level 2, Learning**: did the learner acquire the intended knowledge, skills, or attitudes? (Assessment, observation, knowledge tests.) **Level 3, Behaviour**: is the learner applying what they learned in their role? (Observation, one-to-one supervision, peer feedback.) **Level 4, Results**: has the development activity produced measurable organisational outcomes? (Performance data, quality metrics, customer satisfaction scores.)

At Level 3, the most accessible evaluation levels for a first-line manager are Levels 1, 2, and 3, asking the team member for their reaction to the activity, assessing what they learned through observation and conversation, and tracking whether their behaviour has changed in the relevant areas. Level 4 (organisational results) typically requires longer timeframes and organisational measurement systems beyond the first-line manager's direct control.

## Pass / Merit / Distinction at CMI Level 3

**Pass:** TNA is described. Honey and Mumford's learning styles are named and described. Formal and informal development methods are described. A workplace example is included.

**Merit:** TNA is applied to a specific team member development need, identifying the gap, diagnosing the cause (knowledge/skill deficit vs other cause), and selecting an appropriate development approach. Learning styles are connected to method selection. Kirkpatrick's evaluation levels are applied.

**Distinction:** The student demonstrates evaluative depth, for example: "Following an TNA for a team member struggling with customer complaint handling, I identified that the gap was not primarily a skills deficit but a confidence issue rooted in insufficient feedback. Sending them on a customer service training course would address the Level 10 formal learning component (Kirkpatrick Level 2) but would not address the 70% on-the-job learning gap. The development plan therefore focused on structured coaching conversations after complaint interactions, with specific positive and developmental feedback, plus a paired shadowing period with a high-performing colleague. Within four weeks, complaint resolution rates for this team member improved from 60% to 80% first-contact resolution, a Level 3 (behavioural) and early Level 4 (results) evaluation." This diagnostic, evidence-connected application earns Distinction.

## Common Questions About CMI Unit 307

**Do I need to use Honey and Mumford specifically, or can I use Kolb's learning cycle?**
David Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle (1984), Concrete Experience, Reflective Observation, Abstract Conceptualisation, Active Experimentation, is the theoretical foundation on which Honey and Mumford built their learning styles model. Both are appropriate for Unit 307. Kolb's cycle is useful for explaining the development process itself (experience → reflection → theory → experiment); Honey and Mumford's styles are useful for explaining individual preferences within that cycle. Using Kolb's cycle for the development process LO and Honey and Mumford's styles for the individual preference LO gives comprehensive coverage without duplication.

**What is the difference between coaching and mentoring in Unit 307?**
Coaching and mentoring are frequently confused but have distinct characteristics. Coaching is a structured, goal-focused conversation in which the coach facilitates the learner's own problem-solving and development, the coach's role is to ask questions that develop the learner's thinking, not to provide answers. Mentoring is a relationship in which a more experienced person (the mentor) shares their knowledge, experience, and perspective with a less experienced person (the mentee), it is more advice-giving and experience-transferring than coaching. At Level 3, distinguishing the two and explaining when each is appropriate (coaching for developing problem-solving and judgement; mentoring for sharing experience and navigating organisational culture) demonstrates the Explain depth the unit requires.

**My organisation does not have formal development processes. Can I still write about Unit 307?**
Yes. Unit 307 does not require formal organisational development systems, it asks you to describe and explain approaches to identifying and supporting development. If your organisation lacks formal TNA processes, describe the informal methods you use: observing performance, having development conversations in one-to-ones, identifying when a team member is struggling with a specific task. If your organisation has no formal training budget, describe the informal development opportunities you create: peer learning, stretch assignments, coaching conversations. The unit assesses your understanding of the development manager's role, formal systems are the context, not the requirement.

**How specific does the learning styles application need to be?**
At Merit and Distinction depth, learning styles should be connected to specific team members (described without identifying details if needed) and specific development methods. For example: "My team member who demonstrates Reflector characteristics, taking time to observe before contributing in meetings and preferring to review briefing documents before team discussions, benefits from a development approach that builds in structured reflection time after new tasks. An Activist-oriented immersive training course would not suit their learning preference; a shadowing and structured debrief approach aligns with how they process new information." This specificity is what moves application from Pass to Merit.

**Does Kirkpatrick's evaluation model need to be covered in Unit 307?**
Kirkpatrick's four-level evaluation model is not always explicitly listed in the Unit 307 brief, but the Learning Outcome on evaluating the effectiveness of development activities requires an evaluation framework. Kirkpatrick provides the most accessible and relevant framework for Level 3. Alternative evaluation approaches include: asking learners directly about what they have learned (Reaction and Learning levels); observing whether behaviour has changed (Behaviour level); tracking performance data for changes attributable to the development activity (Results level). Using Kirkpatrick by name and applying its levels demonstrates theoretical grounding; describing the same levels without the framework name still addresses the LO.
