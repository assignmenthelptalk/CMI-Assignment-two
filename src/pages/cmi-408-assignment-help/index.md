## What CMI Unit 408 Covers

Unit 408 addresses equality, diversity, and inclusion as interconnected management responsibilities. The learning outcomes require you to analyse the legal framework governing equality and diversity in the workplace, evaluate the manager's responsibilities for promoting equality and managing diversity, and analyse the conditions that enable an inclusive workplace culture. At Level 4, the Equality Act 2010 must be applied analytically, examining what its provisions mean for management practice, not just naming the protected characteristics.

## The Equality Act 2010 — Level 4 Analyse Depth

The Equality Act 2010 consolidated and strengthened previous equality legislation in England, Scotland, and Wales into a single statute. At Level 3, students describe the nine protected characteristics and the forms of prohibited conduct. At Level 4, the analysis examines what the legislation requires managers to do, how different types of discrimination arise in management practice, and what the duty to make reasonable adjustments means operationally.

**The nine protected characteristics** must be named accurately: Age, Disability, Gender Reassignment, Marriage and Civil Partnership, Pregnancy and Maternity, Race, Religion or Belief, Sex, Sexual Orientation.

**Forms of prohibited conduct at Analyse depth:**

**Direct discrimination**: treating someone less favourably because of a protected characteristic. At Analyse depth: direct discrimination in management practice most commonly occurs in recruitment and promotion decisions, selecting a candidate based on assumptions about their protected characteristic rather than their merit. Analysis examines the mechanisms: unconscious bias (automatic mental shortcuts that associate protected characteristics with assumptions about capability or fit) can produce direct discrimination without conscious discriminatory intent, but the legal test is whether the less favourable treatment is because of the protected characteristic, not whether it was intentional.

**Indirect discrimination**: applying a provision, criterion, or practice that applies equally to all but which has a disproportionate adverse impact on people sharing a protected characteristic, without objective justification. At Analyse depth: indirect discrimination is analytically more complex than direct discrimination. It can arise from seemingly neutral management decisions, requiring all staff to work a specific shift pattern (which disproportionately affects those with caring responsibilities, which affects women disproportionately, indirect sex discrimination); requiring a role to be office-based without operational justification (which may indirectly discriminate against disabled people who need home-working adjustments). The objective justification test is critical: indirect discrimination is not automatically unlawful if the employer can show it is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim.

**Harassment**: unwanted conduct related to a protected characteristic that has the purpose or effect of violating the person's dignity or creating an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating, or offensive environment. At Analyse depth: harassment creates a management responsibility even when the manager is not the harasser. A manager who is aware of harassment occurring within their team and does not act to address it may be found vicariously liable (the employer is liable for harassment by employees if they did not take reasonable steps to prevent it). The management responsibility is both to create conditions that prevent harassment and to respond swiftly and appropriately when it occurs.

**The duty to make reasonable adjustments (disability)**: where a provision, criterion, or practice, or a physical feature of the premises, puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage, the employer must take reasonable steps to avoid the disadvantage. At Analyse depth: "reasonable" is assessed against the size and resources of the employer, the practicality of the adjustment, and its effectiveness. Failure to make reasonable adjustments is a distinct form of disability discrimination, and the request for an adjustment does not need to specify it as a legal claim for the duty to apply. A manager who is aware that a team member's disability creates a disadvantage has a duty to explore adjustments, regardless of whether the team member has formally requested them.

## The Equality and Human Rights Commission (EHRC) and PSED

The Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED) applies to all public sector organisations (NHS, local authorities, central government, maintained schools). Under the PSED, public bodies must have due regard to: eliminating discrimination; advancing equality of opportunity; and build good relations between different groups. This creates a proactive duty, public sector managers must consider equality impact when making decisions, developing policies, and designing services, not only when discrimination is alleged.

## Diversity Management Beyond Legal Compliance — Analyse Depth

At Level 4, Unit 408 requires analysis of diversity as a positive management asset, not only as a legal obligation. The business case for diversity: diverse teams, where members bring different backgrounds, perspectives, experiences, and cognitive approaches, produce better problem-solving, more creative solutions, and more accurate risk assessment when properly managed. The conditions for this are not simply demographic diversity but psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999), team members feeling safe to contribute their distinct perspective without fear of rejection, dismissal, or marginalisation.

The distinction between equality and inclusion is analytically critical at Level 4. **Equality** means treating people fairly and without discrimination. **Diversity** means the presence of difference within the team or organisation. **Inclusion** means creating conditions where all team members feel they genuinely belong and can contribute their full capability. It is possible to have demographic diversity without inclusion, a team that has recruited diverse members but whose culture defaults to the perspective of the dominant group, where some members must mask aspects of their identity to belong, has diversity without inclusion.

At Evaluate depth: evaluate the specific conditions in a management context that are enabling or undermining inclusion, and identify the one change that would most improve inclusive practice. For a team with demographic diversity but low psychological safety, the priority is not further recruitment diversity but creating the conditions in which existing diversity can be expressed and valued.

## Pass / Merit / Distinction

**Pass:** Nine protected characteristics named correctly. Forms of prohibited conduct described. Reasonable adjustments addressed. Business case for diversity included. Workplace examples provided.

**Merit:** Direct and indirect discrimination are analysed with mechanisms, not just defined. The duty to make reasonable adjustments is applied to a specific disability scenario. Equality, diversity, and inclusion are distinguished. Unconscious bias is analysed as a driver of management behaviour.

**Distinction, worked example:** "The organisation's promotion decision-making process contains a structural indirect discrimination risk that the hiring managers are unlikely to be aware of. The requirement for all candidates to have completed a full-time secondment as a prerequisite for band promotion, a provision, criterion, or practice applied equally to all, has a disproportionate adverse impact on part-time employees, who are disproportionately female (indirect sex discrimination) and disproportionately disabled (where full-time secondment is not feasible given health management needs or reasonable adjustment requirements). The objective justification test requires the organisation to demonstrate that the secondment requirement is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim, developing leadership experience. However, if part-time employees can demonstrate equivalent leadership development through flexible secondment arrangements, job shadowing, or extended development in their substantive role, the blanket full-time requirement is not a proportionate means of achieving the aim. The management intervention: review the promotion criteria to ensure the capability developed through secondment can be demonstrated through equivalent experiences, removing the indirect discrimination while preserving the development aim."

## Common Questions About CMI Unit 408

**How is Level 4 Unit 408 different from Level 3 Unit 306?**
At Level 3 (Unit 306), the nine protected characteristics are named, forms of discrimination are described, and reasonable adjustments are introduced at Explain depth. At Level 4 (Unit 408), the analysis examines the mechanisms that produce discrimination in management practice, how unconscious bias generates direct discrimination without conscious intent, how seemingly neutral policies produce indirect discrimination, what conditions enable inclusion rather than just diversity. The Level 4 standard requires examining why discrimination occurs and what managerial action genuinely addresses its mechanisms, not just its symptoms.

**Are Marriage and Civil Partnership protected in the same way as other characteristics?**
No. Marriage and Civil Partnership is a protected characteristic in the Equality Act 2010, but it is protected more narrowly than other characteristics, it applies only to the workplace (employment, not services) and protection is available only to those who are currently married or in a civil partnership, not to those who are single, divorced, or widowed. Direct discrimination on this ground in recruitment and employment is prohibited; however, the indirect discrimination and harassment provisions that apply to most protected characteristics do not extend to Marriage and Civil Partnership. At Level 4, noting this distinction demonstrates accurate legal knowledge rather than assuming all nine characteristics are protected identically.

**What is unconscious bias and how does it relate to the Equality Act?**
Unconscious bias refers to the automatic mental associations and stereotypes that influence judgement without conscious awareness, for example, automatically associating certain roles with particular genders, or making inferences about capability based on age. Unconscious bias can produce discriminatory outcomes that are legally prohibited under the Equality Act even though they were not intentionally discriminatory. The legal test for direct discrimination is whether the treatment was because of a protected characteristic, not whether it was intended. At Level 4, this is the key analytical point: legal liability for discrimination does not require proof of intent, which means that unconscious bias is not an excuse and that management must take active steps to reduce its influence on decision-making.

**Does the Public Sector Equality Duty apply to private sector organisations?**
The PSED applies directly only to public authorities as defined in Schedule 19 of the Equality Act 2010, NHS bodies, local authorities, government departments, maintained schools, and similar organisations. However, private sector organisations that deliver public contracts may be required to demonstrate PSED compliance as a contractual condition. Private sector organisations have general equality law obligations (Equality Act 2010) regardless of the PSED, but the proactive duty to assess equality impact in advance of decisions is a public sector-specific requirement.

**How do I write about equality issues in my workplace without creating problems for myself or my employer?**
At Level 4, anonymisation is appropriate and expected. Refer to "a management decision in my organisation" or "a policy in my sector" rather than naming the organisation or individuals. If the scenario involves ongoing issues, frame your analysis in terms of what the law requires and what management practice should be, rather than making allegations about specific people. Assessment markers are looking for analytical capability, not organisational disclosure. If genuine disclosure concerns exist, construct a scenario based on publicly reported cases (Employment Tribunal decisions are publicly available) and apply the frameworks to that scenario rather than your specific workplace.
