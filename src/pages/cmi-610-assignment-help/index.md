## SERVQUAL — Critically Evaluate

SERVQUAL (Parasuraman, A., Zeithaml, V.A. and Berry, L.L., 1988, 'SERVQUAL: a multiple-item scale for measuring consumer perceptions of service quality', *Journal of Retailing*, 64(1)) is the most widely applied service quality measurement tool in management practice. It measures the gap between customers' **expectations** of a service and their **perceptions** of the service received across five dimensions: **Reliability** (ability to perform the promised service dependably and accurately); **Assurance** (knowledge and courtesy of employees and their ability to inspire trust and confidence); **Tangibles** (physical facilities, equipment, and appearance of personnel); **Empathy** (caring, individualised attention to customers); **Responsiveness** (willingness to help customers and provide prompt service).

**Application**: SERVQUAL at Level 6 is applied as a diagnostic tool for identifying the specific service quality gaps that drive customer dissatisfaction. By surveying customers on both expectations and perceptions across the five dimensions, the senior manager identifies which dimension has the largest negative gap (where perceptions most fall short of expectations) and prioritises service improvement accordingly.

**Critically Evaluate — expectation formation assumption**: SERVQUAL's most fundamental assumption is that service quality can be measured as the gap between pre-experience expectations and post-experience perceptions. Cronin and Taylor (1992, 'Measuring service quality: a reexamination and extension', *Journal of Marketing*, 56(3)) challenged this assumption empirically: they found that service quality perceptions alone (without the expectation gap measurement) were a better predictor of customer satisfaction and repurchase intention than the expectation-perception gap. Their SERVPERF model (service performance only) is simpler to administer and has shown comparable predictive validity. At Level 6, the defensible position requires evaluating whether the expectation-perception gap measurement adds analytical value for the specific service context or whether measuring service performance perceptions alone is sufficient.

**Critically Evaluate — five-dimension universality assumption**: SERVQUAL assumes that the five dimensions (Reliability, Assurance, Tangibles, Empathy, Responsiveness) are appropriate categories for all service contexts. Empirical research has found that the five dimensions do not always emerge as distinct factors in confirmatory factor analysis — some research contexts produce fewer, merged dimensions, suggesting the five-category structure is an assumption rather than a stable empirical finding across all service types. Healthcare service quality, for example, may require dimensions not captured in the original five — clinical outcome quality and safety assurance are service quality dimensions specific to healthcare that SERVQUAL's generic categories do not capture.

**Critically Evaluate — expectation stability assumption**: SERVQUAL assumes that customers hold stable expectations before the service encounter that can be measured by survey. Expectations are in practice dynamic — they are shaped by prior experiences, word-of-mouth, marketing communications, and the service encounter itself. The service encounter shapes expectations as it progresses: a customer whose first contact is positive raises their expectations for subsequent contacts. Measuring expectations before the encounter and perceptions after it assumes a separation that does not fully exist.

## Customer Journey Mapping — Critically Evaluate

Customer journey mapping is a service design tool that visualises the end-to-end customer experience — the sequence of interactions, touchpoints, and emotional states the customer experiences from first awareness through to post-purchase. At Level 6, journey mapping is applied as a diagnostic and design tool for identifying experience gaps, pain points, and moments of truth that disproportionately affect customer experience.

**Critically Evaluate — customer perspective assumption**: journey mapping assumes that the organisation can accurately represent the customer's perspective — that the map constructed by the service design team accurately reflects how customers actually experience the service. Research on service design (Stickdorn, M. et al., 2018, *This Is Service Design Doing*, O'Reilly) consistently finds that organisational representations of customer journeys diverge significantly from customers' actual experience — the organisation's map reflects the intended process, not the experienced journey. At Level 6, this limitation means that journey maps developed internally must be validated through customer research before they can serve as reliable design tools.

**Critically Evaluate — linear journey assumption**: customer journey maps typically present the customer experience as a linear sequence from awareness to purchase to post-purchase. In complex service environments — healthcare pathways, insurance claims, legal services — the customer journey is frequently non-linear: customers re-enter earlier stages, experience multiple parallel journeys simultaneously (family members in a patient journey), or encounter touchpoints that the linear map does not capture. The linear map is a useful simplification that can become a misleading design framework in complex service contexts.

## CX Strategy and the Voice of the Customer

At Level 6, CX strategy addresses how senior managers embed customer experience as a strategic priority — not just measuring service quality but designing the organisational capabilities (systems, culture, data infrastructure) that enable consistent excellent customer experience.

**Net Promoter Score (NPS) — Critically Evaluate**: NPS (Reichheld, F.F., 2003, 'The one number you need to grow', *Harvard Business Review*, 81(12)) measures customer loyalty through a single question: "How likely are you to recommend us to a friend or colleague?" Critically Evaluating NPS at Level 6 requires engaging with the research literature: Keiningham et al. (2007, *Journal of Marketing*, 71(3)) found in a multi-industry study that NPS was not consistently superior to other satisfaction measures in predicting growth, despite Reichheld's claims. The "one number you need" claim is a marketing claim, not a robust empirical finding. NPS has significant cultural bias — average scores vary significantly between countries and industries, making cross-context comparisons unreliable without normalisation.

## Pass / Merit / Distinction

**Pass:** SERVQUAL five dimensions applied to a service context. Customer journey map developed. NPS or CX measurement approach described. Assumptions named. Peer-reviewed sources included.

**Merit:** SERVQUAL's expectation-perception gap assumption examined with Cronin and Taylor (1992) challenge. Journey map's internal perspective limitation addressed. NPS's growth prediction claims evaluated against empirical evidence.

**Distinction — worked example:** "Critically Evaluating the SERVQUAL application to the NHS Trust's patient experience improvement programme reveals a structural limitation in the framework's diagnostic value for this context. The Trust's patient experience survey measures the expectation-perception gap across SERVQUAL's five dimensions — but Cronin and Taylor's (1992) evidence that performance perceptions alone predict satisfaction more reliably than the gap measure suggests that the Trust's survey is adding complexity without improving diagnostic precision. More critically, the five-dimension structure does not adequately capture the clinical quality dimensions that patients weight most heavily in healthcare service evaluation: clinical outcome and safety assurance do not appear as separate dimensions in the standard SERVQUAL framework. The Trust's lowest-scoring SERVQUAL dimension — Empathy — is the correct diagnostic priority, but the action implication differs from commercial service contexts: in healthcare, Empathy deficit is primarily a communication skills and workload pressure issue rather than a customer service training issue. The defensible recommendation is to complement SERVQUAL with a healthcare-specific quality framework (NHS England's Friends and Family Test patient experience data, integrated with FFT comment analysis using thematic coding) to triangulate the quantitative gap measurement with qualitative evidence that identifies the specific interaction types driving Empathy score reductions."

## Common Questions About CMI Unit 610

**What is the difference between SERVQUAL and SERVPERF?**
SERVQUAL (Parasuraman et al., 1988) measures service quality as the gap between customer expectations and perceptions across five dimensions. SERVPERF (Cronin and Taylor, 1992) measures only service performance perceptions — without the expectations measurement. Cronin and Taylor argued that the performance perception alone is a better predictor of customer behaviour than the gap measure, because expectations are unstable and the gap calculation adds measurement error without adding predictive power. At Level 6, Critically Evaluating SERVQUAL requires engaging with Cronin and Taylor's empirical challenge: the question is not whether SERVQUAL is a useful tool (it is) but whether the expectation measurement adds value beyond performance measurement alone in the specific service context.

**Is NPS a reliable measure of customer loyalty?**
NPS's reliability as a customer loyalty predictor is contested in the research literature. Keiningham et al. (2007) conducted a multi-industry study and found that NPS was not consistently superior to other satisfaction measures in predicting revenue growth — directly challenging Reichheld's "one number you need" claim. NPS is a useful, simple metric that correlates with customer loyalty in many contexts. The Level 6 Critically Evaluate issue is with the strength of the causal claim: Reichheld's claim that NPS is the single best predictor of growth is based on selective evidence. NPS is a valuable customer listening tool; it should not replace a comprehensive voice-of-customer measurement system.

**Can Unit 610 be written about NHS patient experience?**
Yes. NHS patient experience is an excellent context for Unit 610. NHS England publishes patient experience data through the NHS Friends and Family Test (FFT), CQC patient surveys, and NHS Digital patient experience datasets. SERVQUAL can be applied to NHS patient experience — mapping the five dimensions to NHS patient experience dimensions (Reliability: appointments kept and tests performed correctly; Assurance: clinical competence and trust in staff; Tangibles: ward environment and equipment; Empathy: compassionate, individualised care; Responsiveness: prompt attention and timely response to concerns). The Critically Evaluate contribution for NHS contexts: examining which SERVQUAL dimensions most closely correlate with NHS patient safety outcomes, and whether the standard SERVQUAL framework adequately captures clinical quality dimensions.

**What is a "moment of truth" in customer journey mapping?**
A moment of truth (coined by Scandinavian Airlines CEO Jan Carlzon, 1987, *Moments of Truth*, Ballinger) is an interaction between a customer and a service organisation that disproportionately shapes the overall service quality perception — positively or negatively. Not all touchpoints in a customer journey are equally important; moments of truth are the high-stakes interactions that customers remember and that drive satisfaction or dissatisfaction outcomes. At Level 6, identifying moments of truth within a customer journey map — and prioritising service improvement investment at those specific touchpoints — demonstrates analytical depth beyond merely mapping the full journey.
