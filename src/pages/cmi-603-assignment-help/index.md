## What CMI Unit 603 Covers

Unit 603 addresses change leadership as a senior management responsibility, the most challenging and highest-stakes management function at senior level. The learning outcomes require you to critically evaluate change management approaches for complex organisational change, evaluate the role of leadership in creating conditions for successful change, and critically evaluate strategies for managing resistance and sustaining change. The Level 6 Critically Evaluate standard means that Kotter and Lewin cannot be applied as if they are uncontested frameworks, their assumptions must be surfaced and their limitations must be named.

## Kotter's 8-Step Change Model — Critically Evaluate

John Kotter's 8-Step Change Model (1996, *Leading Change*, Harvard Business School Press; revised 2014, *Accelerate*, Harvard Business Review Press) provides the most widely applied change management process framework: (1) Create Urgency; (2) Build a Guiding Coalition; (3) Form a Strategic Vision; (4) Enlist a Volunteer Army; (5) Enable Action by Removing Barriers; (6) Generate Short-term Wins; (7) Sustain Acceleration; (8) Institute Change.

**Critically Evaluate, assumptions**: Kotter's model rests on several testable assumptions. Most significantly, the 8-step model assumes **linear sequential progression:** each step must be substantially complete before the next step begins. This assumption is directly challenged by the complexity of large-scale organisational change, where urgency and coalition-building occur simultaneously, where early wins must sometimes be manufactured before the coalition is consolidated, and where the change process frequently requires iteration and return to earlier steps as circumstances evolve. Kotter's own 2014 revision partially acknowledged this by describing a dual operating system in which the sequential process runs alongside normal hierarchical operations, but the revised model still assumes sequential step completion as the primary logic.

**Second critical assumption**: Kotter's model assumes that change is primarily a leadership challenge, that success depends on leader-driven urgency creation, vision formation, and coalition building. Beer and Nohria's (2000) research directly challenges this assumption: their Theory E / Theory O distinction identifies two categorically different theories of change that cannot be combined. Theory E (Economic) is top-down, leader-driven, and urgency-based, consistent with Kotter's model. Theory O (Organizational) is bottom-up, capability-building, and emergent. Beer and Nohria found that organisations attempting to combine Theory E and Theory O approaches simultaneously destroyed more value than those that chose one approach consistently.

**Peer-reviewed evidence**: Kotter's 70% failure rate claim (that 70% of change initiatives fail) is widely cited but lacks rigorous empirical support as cited. Hughes (2011, *Journal of Change Management*, 11(2)) challenged this figure and found that the evidence base for the "70% failure" statistic is anecdotal rather than empirical. Critically Evaluating Kotter at Level 6 requires engaging with this literature, not simply accepting Kotter's claims as established fact.

## Lewin's Force Field Analysis — Critically Evaluate

Lewin's Force Field Analysis (1951) and three-step Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze model provide the foundational theoretical architecture for most change management frameworks, including Kotter's model. At Level 6, Critically Evaluating Lewin means engaging with two levels of limitation.

**First limitation, oversimplification of change dynamics**: the Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze model presents change as a three-phase sequence with a stable frozen state at the beginning and end. Weick (2000, in Weick, K.E. and Quinn, R.E. (1999) 'Organizational change and development', *Annual Review of Psychology*, 50, pp.361–386) critiques this as fundamentally misrepresenting organisational reality, organisations are in continuous change and adaptation, not stable frozen states punctuated by deliberate change. Lewin's model was developed in small group contexts and its application to large, complex organisational change is an assumption rather than an empirically established extension.

**Second limitation, political and cultural complexity**: Lewin's Force Field Analysis identifies driving and restraining forces but treats them as relatively static and commensurable, they can be weighted against each other. In complex organisational change, resistances are not static forces but dynamic social processes: coalitions form and dissolve, political actors shift allegiances as change consequences become clearer, and institutional logics (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983, *American Sociological Review*, 48(2)) produce resistance that is not attributable to identifiable actors but to deeply embedded organisational assumptions.

**Defensible comparative conclusion**: Lewin's Force Field Analysis provides the superior diagnostic framework, the question of which forces drive and resist change in a specific scenario is a more generative analytical starting point than Kotter's sequential process prescription. Kotter's model provides the superior process architecture for leader-driven change in environments where sequential implementation is feasible, typically planned, bounded change with clear leadership authority. For complex, emergent, politically contested change (large-scale NHS transformation, ICS integration, regulatory-driven sector change), neither framework alone is sufficient.

## Beer and Nohria — Theory E and Theory O — Critically Evaluate

Michael Beer and Nitin Nohria's research (2000, 'Cracking the code of change', *Harvard Business Review*, 78(3)) provides the most analytically powerful addition to change management theory for Level 6. They identify two fundamentally different theories of change:

**Theory E (Economic value)**: change driven by maximising shareholder value, typically top-down, planned, incentive-driven, and directed by consultants and senior leadership. Kotter's 8-step model is primarily a Theory E framework.

**Theory O (Organizational capability)**: change driven by building organisational capability and culture, typically bottom-up, emergent, participation-driven, and focused on developing people's capacity to manage continuous change. Lewin's Force Field Analysis can support both Theory E and Theory O diagnostic work.

**The critical finding**: Beer and Nohria found that combining Theory E and Theory O approaches simultaneously, the instinctive management response to wanting both shareholder returns and people engagement, produced the worst outcomes. Organisations that combined both theories explicitly and sequentially (Theory E restructuring first, then Theory O capability-building) produced better results than those combining them simultaneously.

**Critically Evaluate, defensible conclusion for change scenarios**: for a planned, structurally bounded change (system implementation, merger integration with clear financial objectives), Theory E-informed approaches (Kotter) are most applicable. For a continuous improvement or culture change initiative where the change objective is building long-term adaptive capacity, Theory O-informed approaches are more applicable. For the majority of complex NHS transformation scenarios, the evidence supports a sequential rather than simultaneous combination, with explicit attention to which theory is governing which phase of the change.

## Pass / Merit / Distinction

**Pass:** Kotter and Lewin applied to a change scenario. Assumptions named for each. Beer and Nohria's Theory E/O introduced. Peer-reviewed sources included. Advanced management paper format.

**Merit:** Kotter's linear sequencing assumption Critically Evaluated specifically, Hughes (2011) evidence on failure rate statistics engaged. Lewin's oversimplification of change dynamics addressed with Weick's critique. Beer and Nohria's finding on simultaneous combination applied to the specific change scenario.

**Distinction, worked example:** "The Critically Evaluate analysis of the ICS integration change initiative reveals that the change sponsors are attempting to combine Theory E and Theory O approaches simultaneously, a combination that Beer and Nohria's (2000) research found systematically destroys more value than either approach pursued consistently. The explicit merger of financial savings targets (Theory E: shareholder-equivalent: public resource efficiency) with cultural integration and people development objectives (Theory O: capability building) within the same 18-month programme timeline is the primary risk factor in the change design. Kotter's 8-step model, which is structurally a Theory E framework, is being applied to Theory O objectives, its urgency creation and guiding coalition mechanics are calibrated for top-down directed change, not for the emergent participation-driven cultural integration that the ICS integration requires. Lewin's Force Field Analysis exposes the specific restraining forces that the Kotter application is failing to address: not insufficient urgency (Step 1 is well-developed) but inadequate processing of professional identity threat, the restraining force that Beer and Nohria identify as the primary driver of Theory O resistance. The defensible conclusion is that the current change design requires restructuring: Theory E financial savings objectives must be scoped and sequenced separately from Theory O cultural integration work, with explicit acknowledgment that the integration timeline for Theory O objectives is 3–5 years, not 18 months."

## Common Questions About CMI Unit 603

**What is Beer and Nohria's key finding and why does it matter for Unit 603?**
Beer and Nohria's key finding from their study of 12 major change programmes is that Theory E and Theory O cannot be combined simultaneously without destroying value, organisations that tried to pursue financial shareholder returns (Theory E) and build organisational capability (Theory O) at the same time, through the same change programme, produced the worst outcomes in their study. This matters for Unit 603 because most real-world change programmes attempt exactly this combination. Critically Evaluating the change scenario in your brief requires examining whether it is attempting a Theory E/O combination, and if so, whether the Beer and Nohria evidence supports the approach or challenges it.

**Does Kotter himself acknowledge the limitations of the 8-step model?**
Kotter's 2014 revision (*Accelerate*, Harvard Business Review Press) introduced the dual operating system concept, acknowledging that the sequential 8-step model does not adequately capture how change operates in fast-moving environments. The dual operating system proposes that organisations need to run the 8-step change process as a parallel network alongside the normal hierarchical management system. This revision partially concedes the linear sequencing criticism but does not resolve it fully, the 8 steps remain the primary logic even in the revised model. At Level 6, referencing both the 1996 original and the 2014 revision demonstrates awareness of the model's evolution and shows engagement with the theoretical development of the framework.

**Is Lewin's model really as simple as three steps?**
Lewin's contribution to change management is more theoretically sophisticated than the Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze summary suggests. His field theory, force field analysis, and action research contributions each add analytical depth. However, the three-step model as it is typically applied in organisations is genuinely oversimplified. Burnes (2004, *Management Decision*, 42(7/8)) provides a revisionist reading of Lewin that argues his work was misrepresented by later management theorists, and that Lewin's own approach to change was more complex and participatory than the textbook model suggests. Engaging with Burnes at Level 6 demonstrates awareness of the secondary literature on Lewin, which strengthens the Critically Evaluate analysis.

**What is the 70% failure rate statistic in change management and can I use it?**
The 70% change failure rate is widely attributed to Kotter (1996) and repeated throughout the change management literature. Hughes (2011, *Journal of Change Management*, 11(2)) traced this statistic and found that its origins are anecdotal, it is not supported by rigorous empirical research. At Level 6, citing the 70% failure rate without acknowledging Hughes's challenge risks appearing to accept an empirically contested claim as established fact. The Critically Evaluate standard requires engaging with this limitation: the statistic may capture a real pattern of change difficulty, but its magnitude and empirical basis are not established with the certainty the management literature implies.

**How does Unit 603 relate to Unit 709 at Level 7?**
Unit 709 at Level 7 (Leading Strategic Change) requires Critically Analyse depth, engaging with the academic debate between Beer and Nohria, Pettigrew et al., and Weick as competing theoretical traditions, not just Critically Evaluating individual frameworks. Level 7 additionally requires 15–20 primary sources (journal articles and monographs) and an original synthesis position. Unit 603 at Level 6 provides excellent preparation for Unit 709: a student who can Critically Evaluate Kotter, Lewin, and Beer and Nohria at Level 6 has built the analytical vocabulary needed to engage with the academic debate between them at Level 7.
