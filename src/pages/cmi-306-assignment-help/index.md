## What CMI Unit 306 Covers

Unit 306 addresses equality, diversity, and inclusion as a first-line management responsibility — not merely a compliance function. The learning outcomes require you to describe the legal framework for equality in the workplace, explain the first-line manager's role in promoting equality and inclusion, and describe the consequences of failing to meet equality obligations. ## The Equality Act 2010 and the Nine Protected Characteristics

The Equality Act 2010 is the primary UK legislation governing equality in employment and service provision. It consolidates and replaces previous legislation including the Sex Discrimination Act 1975, the Race Relations Act 1976, and the Disability Discrimination Act 1995. The Act makes it unlawful to discriminate against a person in employment on the basis of any of the nine protected characteristics:

1. **Age** — covers all ages; not restricted to older workers
2. **Disability** — a physical or mental impairment that has a substantial and long-term adverse effect on the ability to carry out normal day-to-day activities
3. **Gender Reassignment** — people who are proposing to, are undergoing, or have undergone gender reassignment
4. **Marriage and Civil Partnership** — protection against discrimination in employment (note: this characteristic does not apply in all legal contexts)
5. **Pregnancy and Maternity** — covers pregnancy, maternity leave, and breastfeeding
6. **Race** — includes colour, nationality, and ethnic or national origin
7. **Religion or Belief** — includes religious belief, philosophical belief, and lack of belief
8. **Sex** — covers both men and women
9. **Sexual Orientation** — covers gay, lesbian, bisexual, and heterosexual orientation

At Describe depth: name all nine protected characteristics with a brief description of each. At Explain depth: explain what the Act means for a first-line manager's daily decisions — recruitment, task allocation, performance management, discipline, and access to development opportunities must all be free from discrimination on the basis of any protected characteristic.

## Direct and Indirect Discrimination

The Equality Act 2010 defines four types of prohibited conduct. At Level 3, the two most important to understand are direct discrimination and indirect discrimination.

**Direct discrimination** occurs when a person is treated less favourably than another person would be treated in a comparable situation because of a protected characteristic. Examples: not shortlisting a job candidate because they are over 50; refusing to offer flexible working to a woman with childcare responsibilities but not raising the same objection with a man in comparable circumstances; dismissing a team member because of their religion.

**Indirect discrimination** occurs when a provision, criterion, or practice (PCP) that applies equally to all employees puts people with a particular protected characteristic at a particular disadvantage compared to others, and the PCP is not justified as a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim. Example: requiring all staff to work on Saturdays as a condition of a promotion applies equally to all — but disproportionately disadvantages Jewish or Seventh-day Adventist employees for whom Saturday is a religious rest day. If this requirement is not operationally necessary, it may constitute indirect discrimination on grounds of religion.

The distinction matters for first-line managers because indirect discrimination is often unintentional — a manager who sets a policy they believe is neutral may be indirectly discriminating without realising it. This is why inclusive practice requires active reflection on the potential impact of policies and decisions, not just the absence of deliberate unfair treatment.

Two additional conduct types: **Harassment** — unwanted conduct related to a protected characteristic that has the purpose or effect of violating dignity or creating an intimidating, hostile, degrading, humiliating, or offensive environment. **Victimisation** — subjecting a person to a detriment because they have raised a complaint or supported someone else's complaint of discrimination.

## The Equality Duty and Reasonable Adjustments

The Public Sector Equality Duty (PSED), introduced by the Equality Act 2010, requires public sector organisations (including NHS Trusts) to have due regard to the need to eliminate discrimination, advance equality of opportunity, and foster good relations between people with different protected characteristics. This duty applies to the organisation as a whole, but first-line managers in public sector settings contribute to its fulfilment through their day-to-day management decisions.

The duty to make **reasonable adjustments** for disabled employees is a specific legal obligation: where a provision, criterion, or practice, or a physical feature of premises, puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage compared to non-disabled colleagues, the employer must take reasonable steps to remove or reduce that disadvantage. At first-line manager level, reasonable adjustment responsibilities include: adjusting work schedules; modifying task allocation; providing additional equipment or support; adjusting performance standards during a disability-related period; and facilitating phased return to work.

## Inclusive Management Practice

Beyond legal compliance, inclusive management practice at first-line level requires active behaviours that create an environment where all team members can contribute their full capability regardless of protected characteristic.

Inclusive practice includes: using inclusive language that does not exclude or stereotype; actively seeking the perspectives of all team members in team discussions rather than defaulting to the loudest voices; being consistent in the application of policies (such as flexible working, performance management, and development opportunities) so that no group receives systematically different treatment; being alert to and addressing microaggressions — subtle behaviours or comments that, while not meeting the legal threshold of harassment, undermine the psychological safety of team members from minority groups; and modelling inclusive behaviour so that team norms reflect equality as a genuine value rather than a compliance obligation.

## Pass / Merit / Distinction at CMI Level 3

**Pass:** Nine protected characteristics are named. Direct and indirect discrimination are described. Inclusive management behaviours are described. A workplace example is included.

**Merit:** The distinction between direct and indirect discrimination is clearly explained with a workplace example for each. The first-line manager's specific responsibilities under the Equality Act are explained, not just the Act's general provisions. Reasonable adjustments are explained with a practical example.

**Distinction:** The student applies the frameworks diagnostically — for example: "A recent policy change in my team requiring all team members to attend a mandatory Thursday evening team meeting creates a potential indirect discrimination concern. Several team members are primary carers with family responsibilities that prevent Thursday evening availability. While the policy is applied equally to all, it places team members with caring responsibilities — predominantly women and those with disability-related care obligations — at a particular disadvantage. Without a clear operational justification for the specific timing, this policy may constitute indirect discrimination on grounds of sex and disability. The appropriate management response is to review whether the meeting purpose could be achieved at an alternative time, or whether virtual attendance options would remove the disadvantage." This regulatory application to a real workplace scenario is the Distinction quality.

## Common Questions About CMI Unit 306

**Do I need to memorise all nine protected characteristics for the Unit 306 assignment?**
Yes — accurately naming all nine protected characteristics is a basic factual requirement for the Learning Outcome that addresses the legal framework. A common mistake is listing eight and omitting one (Marriage and Civil Partnership and Gender Reassignment are the most frequently omitted). Provide a brief description of each characteristic and note any important distinctions — for example, that Age protects all age groups, not just older workers, and that Marriage and Civil Partnership only applies in the employment context, not in all legal contexts.

**What is the difference between equality and diversity, and why does the distinction matter?**
Equality is the legal and ethical requirement to treat people fairly and without unlawful discrimination — ensuring that people with protected characteristics are not disadvantaged by organisational decisions or practices. Diversity is the recognition and valuing of difference — the range of visible and non-visible differences in a team or organisation including protected characteristics but also wider dimensions of background, experience, and perspective. Inclusion is the active creation of an environment where diversity is genuinely valued and all team members can contribute fully. A diverse team that is not managed inclusively may have representation without belonging — the distinction matters because demographic diversity without inclusive culture does not deliver the performance, creativity, and retention benefits that the inclusion research evidence supports.

**What are microaggressions and do I need to cover them in Unit 306?**
Microaggressions are brief, everyday exchanges that send denigrating messages to members of marginalised groups, often unintentionally. Examples include asking a person of colour where they are "really" from; assuming the only woman in a meeting is the note-taker; commenting on a disabled team member's "bravery" in performing ordinary tasks. While not typically defined in the Equality Act 2010 (they may not meet the legal threshold for harassment), microaggressions are part of inclusive management practice at Level 3 and relevant to explaining why the absence of deliberate discrimination does not guarantee an inclusive environment. Including them demonstrates depth of understanding beyond the legislative minimum.

**Is unconscious bias relevant to Unit 306?**
Unconscious bias — automatic, implicit associations and stereotypes that affect judgement without conscious awareness — is relevant to explaining how indirect discrimination occurs despite the absence of deliberate intent. A manager who does not consciously discriminate may still systematically shortlist, promote, or allocate development opportunities in ways that favour people who look or sound like themselves, due to affinity bias. At Level 3, introducing the concept of unconscious bias to explain why equality requires active management — not just the absence of deliberate discrimination — adds depth to the Explain command verb requirement.

**My organisation operates outside the NHS — is Unit 306 still relevant?**
Unit 306 applies to all first-line managers regardless of sector — the Equality Act 2010 applies across all employment contexts. NHS students can contextualise within WRES/WDES obligations; retail, hospitality, logistics, and commercial students should contextualise within their own sector's equality obligations and workforce demographics. The legal framework is the same; the sector-specific application and examples will differ.
