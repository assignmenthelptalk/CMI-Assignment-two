## What CMI Unit 616 Covers

Unit 616 addresses research methodology as a senior management competency, not as a technical academic skill but as the capability to design, evaluate, and apply evidence to management decision-making. The learning outcomes require you to critically evaluate qualitative and quantitative research methodologies for management inquiry, analyse the epistemological assumptions underlying different research approaches, and critically evaluate the quality, validity, and applicability of research evidence for specific management decisions. At Level 6, the standard requires examining what each methodology assumes about the nature of knowledge and the kind of evidence it can produce.

## Research Paradigms — Critically Evaluate

The most fundamental distinction in research methodology at Level 6 is between positivist and interpretivist paradigms, the philosophical positions that underpin quantitative and qualitative research respectively.

**Positivism** assumes that social reality exists independently of the observer and can be measured objectively. It produces quantitative research, surveys, experiments, statistical analysis, that seeks generalisable laws and causal relationships. The natural sciences model of inquiry is the template.

**Interpretivism** assumes that social reality is constructed through human interpretation and meaning-making, that organisational phenomena cannot be understood independently of the meanings that participants attach to them. It produces qualitative research, interviews, ethnography, document analysis, that seeks to understand the subjective experience and meaning-structures of organisational life. The humanities model of inquiry is the template.

**Critical realism** (Bhaskar, R., 1975, *A Realist Theory of Science*, Leeds Books) provides a third paradigm that attempts to reconcile positivism and interpretivism: social reality has structures and mechanisms that exist independently of our observation, but those structures are not directly observable, they must be inferred from their observable effects through both quantitative and qualitative inquiry. Critical realism is increasingly influential in management research and provides the philosophical basis for mixed-methods approaches.

**Critically Evaluate, paradigm assumption**: the positivist/interpretivist distinction is not merely a methodological preference but rests on different ontological and epistemological assumptions. Critically Evaluating research methodology at Level 6 requires understanding which paradigm the chosen methodology inhabits and what that implies about the kind of knowledge it can produce. A quantitative survey of staff engagement can tell you what proportion of staff report high engagement; it cannot tell you what engagement means to different staff groups or why engagement patterns vary between teams. Interpretivist qualitative inquiry can address the meaning and mechanism questions that quantitative research cannot.

## Quantitative Research Methods — Critically Evaluate

Quantitative methods in management research include surveys, experiments, quasi-experiments, and statistical analysis of secondary data. At Level 6, the Critically Evaluate analysis examines what quantitative methods can and cannot tell the senior manager.

**Surveys**: the most commonly used quantitative tool in management research. Survey validity depends on: measurement validity (whether the questions measure what they claim to measure); response rate (whether the respondent sample is representative of the target population); and social desirability bias (respondents answering what they believe is the expected or socially acceptable response rather than their actual views). NHS staff surveys are a primary example: response rate variation between organisations (from 30% to 70%) makes cross-organisational comparison problematic, high-responding organisations may have systematically different staff engagement characteristics than low-responding organisations, confounding the comparison.

**Randomised controlled trials (RCTs)**: the gold standard of causal inference in experimental research. RCTs randomly allocate participants to intervention and control groups, eliminating selection bias and enabling causal claims. In management research, RCTs are rarely feasible, it is not possible to randomly allocate organisations to leadership styles, merger strategies, or culture change interventions. The absence of RCT evidence for most management interventions means that causal claims in management research are typically based on weaker evidence designs.

**Critically Evaluate, causation vs correlation**: the most significant quality issue in quantitative management research is the confusion of correlation with causation. Studies showing that high-performing organisations use transformational leadership, or that diverse teams produce better financial outcomes, are typically correlational, they do not establish that transformational leadership causes performance or that diversity causes financial outcomes. Selection effects (better-performing organisations may attract more transformational leaders), third-variable effects (both transformational leadership and performance may be caused by a third factor such as organisational resources), and reverse causation (successful organisations may be more able to invest in leadership development) are alternative explanations that correlation analysis cannot rule out.

## Qualitative Research Methods — Critically Evaluate

Qualitative methods in management research include semi-structured interviews, focus groups, ethnography, case studies, and document analysis. At Level 6, the Critically Evaluate analysis examines what qualitative methods can and cannot tell the senior manager.

**Semi-structured interviews**: the most widely used qualitative method in management research. They provide rich, contextualised accounts of participants' experiences, interpretations, and meaning-making. At Level 6, Critically Evaluating interview research requires examining: sampling (were participants selected to represent a range of perspectives, or were they convenience-selected in ways that may bias the findings?); interviewer effect (how may the researcher's role, identity, and relationship with participants have influenced the data?); and interpretation (how were themes identified from the data, through inductive thematic analysis or through a pre-existing theoretical framework?).

**Reflexivity**: interpretive qualitative research at Level 6 requires reflexivity, the researcher's acknowledgment of how their own position, perspective, and assumptions may have shaped the research design, data collection, and interpretation. A senior manager researching their own organisation's change experience is both insider (with privileged access and contextual understanding) and subject (with positions, relationships, and interests that may affect interpretation). Reflexivity does not eliminate this influence but makes it transparent and analytically manageable.

## Evidence-Based Management — Critically Evaluate

Evidence-based management (EBM), the application of the best available evidence to management decisions, is the senior management competency that Unit 616 ultimately addresses. Pfeffer and Sutton (2006, 'Evidence-based management', *Harvard Business Review*, 84(1)) argued that management practice is characterised by a widespread "knowing-doing gap", managers know evidence-based approaches but do not apply them. At Level 6, the Critically Evaluate analysis of EBM examines both why evidence is underused in management decision-making and what quality of evidence should inform senior management decisions.

**GRADE evidence hierarchy** (adapted for management research): systematic reviews of RCTs provide the strongest evidence for causal claims; cohort studies and non-experimental research provide weaker evidence; expert opinion provides the weakest evidence. Most management decisions are supported by evidence from the weakest parts of this hierarchy, expert opinion, case studies, and correlational survey research. Critically Evaluating the evidence base for a specific management strategy requires identifying where on the evidence hierarchy the available evidence sits and what that implies for confidence in the causal claims.

## Pass / Merit / Distinction

**Pass:** Qualitative and quantitative methodologies described and compared. Positivist and interpretivist paradigms introduced. Evidence quality criteria applied to research examples. Peer-reviewed sources included.

**Merit:** Paradigm assumptions examined, what each assumes about the nature of knowledge and what each can and cannot tell the senior manager. Causation vs correlation limitation in quantitative research examined. Reflexivity in qualitative research addressed. EBM evidence hierarchy applied.

**Distinction, worked example:** "Critically Evaluating the evidence base for the Trust's planned introduction of structured nursing ward rounds, based on a cited quality improvement report showing a 23% reduction in call-bell usage in a single acute medical ward, reveals multiple research quality limitations that the policy decision is not acknowledging. The single-site, non-randomised design is unable to distinguish between the intervention effect (structured rounds) and selection effects (a particularly engaged ward team who also increased other care improvements contemporaneously with the rounds introduction). The absence of a control ward eliminates the ability to attribute the call-bell reduction to the intervention rather than to concurrent time trends (seasonal variation, staffing stability changes during the study period). The correlational design, as Shadish, Cook and Campbell (2002) note, is systematically prone to threats to internal validity, specifically, history effects and maturation effects, that the single-site design cannot control. The defensible recommendation for the Trust's board is not to halt the policy development but to commission a pilot implementation with an embedded evaluation design that includes a matched control ward, improving the evidential quality from a single case report to a quasi-experimental design with greater internal validity before the Trust commits to system-wide implementation."

## Common Questions About CMI Unit 616

**What is the difference between positivism and interpretivism in management research?**
Positivism assumes that social reality exists independently of the observer and can be measured objectively, producing quantitative research that seeks generalisable findings and causal laws. Interpretivism assumes that social reality is constructed through human meaning-making and cannot be understood independently of the participants' subjective experience, producing qualitative research that seeks to understand meaning, context, and process. The practical difference for management research: a positivist study of leadership effectiveness would measure leadership behaviours quantitatively and correlate them with performance metrics; an interpretivist study would interview managers and followers to understand how they experience leadership and what it means in their specific context. Neither approach alone provides a complete account of most management phenomena, which is the basis for mixed-methods research designs.

**What is reflexivity in qualitative research and why does it matter at Level 6?**
Reflexivity is the researcher's systematic reflection on how their own position, assumptions, and relationships with participants may have influenced the research process and findings. It is particularly important in qualitative research, where the researcher is the primary instrument of data collection and interpretation. A senior manager researching their own organisation's change experience holds an insider position that provides rich access but also creates interpretation risks: they may selectively attend to evidence that confirms their existing understanding, and participants may shape their responses based on the researcher's organisational role. Reflexivity does not eliminate these influences but makes them transparent, enabling the reader to assess how the researcher's position may have shaped the findings. At Level 6, demonstrating reflexive awareness in research design and execution is a marker of methodological sophistication.

At Level 7, the Strategic Management Research Paper requires the student to design and conduct a piece of original management research, developing a research question, selecting and justifying a research methodology, collecting and analysing data, and presenting evidence-based findings and recommendations. Unit 616 provides the methodological literacy needed to make these choices at Level 7: understanding what qualitative and quantitative methods can and cannot tell, what paradigm underpins the chosen methodology, how to assess evidence quality, and how to address reflexivity and validity. Students who study Unit 616 before their Level 7 research paper consistently find the methodological decisions easier to make and justify.

**What is evidence-based management and how does it differ from evidence-based medicine?**
Evidence-based medicine (EBM) was developed in the 1990s (Sackett et al., 1996, *British Medical Journal*, 312) as the systematic use of the best available clinical evidence in clinical decision-making, with a clear evidence hierarchy (RCTs and systematic reviews at the apex). Evidence-based management (Pfeffer and Sutton, 2006) applies the same principle to management decisions, using the best available research evidence rather than managerial intuition, fashion, or convention. The practical difference: clinical RCT evidence is relatively abundant for many treatment decisions; management research evidence is more mixed in quality, less frequently based on experimental designs, and more context-dependent. Evidence-based management at Level 6 does not require RCT-quality evidence for every decision, it requires systematic appraisal of the available evidence, acknowledgment of its quality limitations, and integration of research evidence with experiential knowledge and stakeholder values.
