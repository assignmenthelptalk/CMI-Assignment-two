## What CMI Unit 601 Covers

Unit 601 establishes the management and leadership theory foundation for the Level 6 qualification at Critically Evaluate depth. The learning outcomes require you to critically evaluate management and leadership theories in an organisational context, analyse how leadership behaviours affect organisational performance, and critically evaluate different leadership styles for specific senior management contexts. The Level 6 standard is categorically different from Level 5: at Level 5, naming a framework's limitations achieves Distinction; at Level 6, naming limitations is the Pass baseline.

## Distributed Leadership — Critically Evaluate

James Spillane's distributed leadership framework (2004, *Distributed Leadership*, Jossey-Bass; and Spillane, J.P., 2006, *Distributed Leadership*, Jossey-Bass) argues that leadership is not concentrated in a single positional leader but is distributed across multiple individuals who collectively contribute to organisational direction and practice. Leadership, on this account, is a property of activity and interaction rather than of individuals.

**Application**: distributed leadership at Level 6 means analysing how leadership practice is shared across a senior team, where authority, expertise, and initiative are distributed rather than centralised in the most senior role.

**Critically Evaluate, assumptions and limitations**: distributed leadership rests on two critical assumptions that must be examined. First, it assumes that leadership functions can be distributed without accountability fragmentation, that when multiple people lead different aspects of the organisation's work, collective accountability is maintained. In practice, this assumption is frequently violated: distributed leadership without clear governance structures produces situations where everyone leads and no one is accountable. Spillane's research was primarily conducted in school settings; the generalisability to large, complex organisations with more hierarchical governance requirements is an assumption the framework does not fully address.

Second, distributed leadership assumes that those to whom leadership is distributed have the capacity, motivation, and authority to lead effectively in their domain. Where organisational culture suppresses initiative, where structural authority remains centralised despite rhetorical distribution, or where the skills required for distributed leadership have not been developed, the framework describes an aspiration rather than a functioning system.

**Competing frameworks**: situational leadership (Hersey and Blanchard) provides a competing account that is contingency-based rather than distribution-based, the appropriate leadership style depends on the follower's readiness and capability, not on a principle of distribution. For senior management contexts where follower readiness varies significantly across the team (some direct reports are highly capable and motivated, others require direction), situational leadership provides more granular guidance than distributed leadership's blanket distribution premise.

**Defensible conclusion**: distributed leadership is most applicable in senior management contexts where the organisational work is genuinely complex and interdependent, where no single leader can hold all required expertise, and where governance structures maintain accountability through transparent reporting mechanisms rather than centralised control. In organisations with immature governance, centralised culture, or significant capability gaps among direct reports, distributed leadership as described by Spillane is unlikely to function as intended without prior structural and cultural investment.

## Situational Leadership — Critically Evaluate

Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard's Situational Leadership model (1969, revised in Blanchard, K. et al., 1985, *Leadership and the One Minute Manager*, William Morrow) argues that effective leaders adapt their style to the follower's readiness level, varying their task behaviour (direction) and relationship behaviour (support) according to the follower's development level (D1–D4).

**Critically Evaluate, assumptions**: the model assumes that leadership style can be reliably adapted across a senior manager's direct reports, that the same individual can switch from Directing (S1) to Delegating (S4) effectively within a short timeframe. Cognitive and behavioural flexibility of this kind is demanding and may not match the natural style range of most leaders. The model also assumes that follower readiness is measurable and relatively stable, in complex senior management environments, readiness is dynamic and often context-specific: a direct report may be D4 (high competence, high commitment) in their technical domain and D1 in a newly assigned strategic role. The model provides limited guidance for this intra-individual complexity.

**Defensible conclusion**: situational leadership provides the most operationally useful guidance for Unit 601 senior management contexts where the diversity of direct reports' development levels is significant, typical in newly constituted senior teams or in roles with wide spans of control. Its limitations (cognitive style-switching demands, static readiness assessment) require the senior manager to treat the model as a diagnostic rather than a prescription.

## Servant Leadership — Critically Evaluate

Robert Greenleaf's servant leadership concept (1977, *Servant Leadership*, Paulist Press) inverts conventional leadership assumptions: the leader's primary purpose is to serve the needs of followers, enabling their growth and effectiveness, rather than to direct followers toward the leader's goals. Key servant leader attributes include listening, empathy, stewardship, commitment to people's growth, and building community.

**Critically Evaluate, assumptions**: servant leadership rests on the assumption that the primary source of organisational effectiveness is follower wellbeing and development, and that leaders who prioritise service over direction will produce better outcomes. This assumption has some empirical support in stable, high-trust environments. However, it is challenged in conditions of urgency, crisis, or low follower readiness, where direction rather than service is the most critical leadership function. The model also rests on an assumption about leader motivation (genuine desire to serve) that is difficult to verify externally and that may not characterise most senior management contexts.

**Critically Evaluate, alternatives**: transformational leadership (Bass and Avolio) provides a competing framework that shares servant leadership's focus on follower development (Individualised Consideration) but situates it within a framework that also addresses direction, vision, and inspirational motivation. Transformational leadership is more applicable to senior management contexts requiring both development and direction.

## Pass / Merit / Distinction

**Pass:** Three leadership frameworks applied to a senior management context. Assumptions of each framework named. A preferred framework identified with justification. At least 12 sources with peer-reviewed journals included.

**Merit:** Assumptions examined with specificity, the precise conditions under which each framework breaks down. Competing frameworks evaluated against each other in the same scenario. The conclusion is defensible against critique.

**Distinction, worked example:** "Spillane's distributed leadership framework provides the most theoretically sophisticated account of how leadership operates in a complex NHS Trust environment where no single Director of Operations can hold all the clinical, financial, governance, and workforce expertise required for effective leadership of a multi-specialty division. However, the critical evaluation reveals that distributed leadership's central assumption, that accountability is maintained through distributed practice, is incompatible with the NHS's accountability architecture, in which named Executive Directors carry statutory personal accountability to the Board and CQC. A form of distributed leadership in which leadership practice is distributed while statutory accountability remains personal is not the distributed leadership that Spillane describes. The defensible conclusion is that Hersey and Blanchard's situational leadership provides more operationally applicable guidance for the specific context in the brief, because it acknowledges that the senior manager must personally adapt to their direct reports' readiness levels rather than assume that distributing leadership functions solves the fundamental challenge of diverse follower development needs. The contribution of Spillane is to the governance design question (how is leadership structured across the senior team?), while Hersey and Blanchard addresses the daily practice question (how should this specific senior manager lead this specific direct report today?). Both are required; neither is sufficient."

## Common Questions About CMI Unit 601

**What makes Critically Evaluate different from the Level 5 Evaluate standard?**
At Level 5, Evaluate requires applying a framework, identifying its usefulness and limitations in the specific context, and forming a judgement. Naming limitations is Distinction-level work at Level 5. At Level 6, naming limitations is the Pass baseline, the starting point, not the finish line. Critically Evaluate additionally requires: (1) examining the theoretical assumptions the framework rests on, not just its practical limitations; (2) evaluating competing frameworks against each other in the same scenario; (3) reaching a position that is defensible against a critical challenge, not a balanced "both frameworks have strengths and weaknesses" summary but a conclusion that commits to a view and defends it.

**Is peer-reviewed journal referencing mandatory at Level 6?**
Yes. A Level 6 bibliography composed exclusively of ManagementDirect and textbooks will not support Critically Evaluate at Pass standard. Peer-reviewed journals provide the theoretical claims and empirical evidence needed to evaluate assumptions rather than simply describe frameworks. For Unit 601, key peer-reviewed sources include: Spillane, J.P. (2004) 'Distributed leadership and information technology', *Educational Leadership*; Bass, B.M. and Avolio, B.J. (1994) *Improving Organizational Effectiveness Through Transformational Leadership*, Sage; Hersey, P. and Blanchard, K.H. (1969) 'Life cycle theory of leadership', *Training and Development Journal*, 23(5), pp.26–34.

**How is Unit 601 different from Unit 401 (Level 4) and Unit 501 (Level 5)?**
Unit 401 at Level 4 applies Blake Mouton and Tannenbaum-Schmidt at Analyse depth. Unit 501 at Level 5 applies transformational and transactional leadership at Evaluate depth. Unit 601 at Level 6 applies distributed, situational, and servant leadership frameworks at Critically Evaluate depth, examining theoretical assumptions, engaging with peer-reviewed research on each framework, and reaching a defensible conclusion about which is most applicable for the specific senior management context. The intellectual engagement is qualitatively different: Level 6 requires the student to identify what each framework assumes rather than simply what it recommends.

**What is the difference between distributed leadership and delegated authority?**
Delegation involves a leader with formal authority assigning specific tasks or decisions to a direct report, the authority flows from the leader and can be recalled. Distributed leadership, as Spillane describes it, is not delegation. It is the observation that leadership functions (direction, expertise, sense-making) are naturally distributed across multiple actors in a complex organisation, regardless of formal authority structures. The distinction matters at Level 6: students who describe distributed leadership as "giving authority to team members" have described delegation, not Spillane's concept. The Critically Evaluate standard requires engaging with what Spillane actually argues, that distributed leadership is an observable property of practice in complex organisations, not a management style choice.

**Do I need to use all three frameworks (Spillane, Hersey-Blanchard, Greenleaf) or can I choose?**
The Learning Outcomes determine which frameworks are required. Most Unit 601 briefs address multiple leadership frameworks, check whether your brief names specific frameworks or allows student selection. If the brief specifies distributed, situational, and servant leadership, all three must be addressed. If the brief allows framework selection, choosing three frameworks you can Critically Evaluate with genuine depth (engaging with their assumptions and peer-reviewed evidence) is more effective than selecting three frameworks you can only describe and apply.
