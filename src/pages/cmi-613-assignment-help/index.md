## What CMI Unit 613 Covers

Unit 613 addresses equality, diversity and inclusion as an integrated senior management strategic responsibility. The learning outcomes require you to critically evaluate EDI frameworks for senior management strategy, analyse the systemic conditions that produce and sustain inequality in organisations, and critically evaluate approaches to creating genuine inclusion beyond legal compliance. The Level 6 standard requires the student to move beyond the Equality Act 2010 as the primary frame — legal compliance is the starting point, not the conclusion.

## The Equality Act 2010 — Critically Evaluate as a Floor

At Level 4 (Unit 408), the Equality Act 2010 is applied as the primary framework: nine protected characteristics named, forms of prohibited conduct analysed, reasonable adjustments addressed. At Level 6, the Equality Act is Critically Evaluated as a floor — its limitations as an EDI strategy framework named, and its structural incapacity to produce genuine inclusion identified.

**Limitation 1 — protected characteristic individualism**: the Equality Act protects individuals against discriminatory treatment based on specified protected characteristics. It does not address the structural and systemic conditions that produce group-level disadvantage — patterns of underrepresentation, pay gaps, promotion disparities — that exist without any identifiable individual discriminating. A senior management team that is demographically homogeneous is not necessarily in breach of the Equality Act; the Act does not require diverse representation, only the absence of discriminatory process. At Level 6, this distinction is the central analytical contribution: the Equality Act addresses discriminatory treatment; inclusion strategy addresses structural conditions.

**Limitation 2 — single-axis protection**: the Equality Act protects each characteristic independently — race discrimination is a separate legal category from sex discrimination. This creates a structural gap for people who experience discrimination at the intersection of multiple characteristics — a Black woman does not experience race discrimination that is separable from sex discrimination; her experiences at the intersection are not adequately captured by either race or sex discrimination categories alone.

**Kimberlé Crenshaw's intersectionality**: Crenshaw (1989, 'Demarginalizing the intersection of race and sex', *University of Chicago Legal Forum*, 140, pp.139–167) developed intersectionality as the theoretical framework for understanding how multiple systems of disadvantage interact and compound at the intersection of identity categories. At Level 6, intersectionality is the peer-reviewed theoretical contribution that exceeds what the Equality Act captures — and that the senior manager must apply to understand the experience of team members whose identity intersects multiple protected characteristics.

## Systemic Inclusion Frameworks — Critically Evaluate

Systemic inclusion addresses the structural, cultural, and procedural conditions that produce and sustain inequality in organisations — not just the absence of individual discrimination but the presence of positive conditions for belonging and contribution from all.

**The inclusion continuum**: Katz and Miller (2012) describe an inclusion continuum from Exclusion (active or passive) through Tokenism (representation without voice or influence) through Assimilation (presence required to conform to the dominant culture norm) to Reweaving (genuine inclusion where the organisation's culture is shaped by all its members). At Level 6, Critically Evaluating where an organisation sits on this continuum — and identifying the structural conditions that maintain it at that position — is the analytical contribution that legal compliance analysis cannot provide.

**Critically Evaluate — diversity vs inclusion distinction**: the evidence on diversity without inclusion is clear: diverse teams do not produce better outcomes unless inclusion conditions enable team members to contribute their distinct perspectives (Rock and Grant, 2016, *Harvard Business Review*; Edmondson, 1999 on psychological safety). The diversity-performance link depends on inclusion as the mediating condition. At Level 6, this is the defensible analytical position: demographic diversity is a necessary but not sufficient condition for the performance benefits attributed to diverse teams.

**NHS-specific systemic frameworks**: the Workforce Race Equality Standard (WRES) and Workforce Disability Equality Standard (WDES) are NHS-specific systemic measurement frameworks that go beyond Equality Act compliance to measure representation, experience, and opportunity at each NHS workforce band level. For NHS Level 6 students, WRES and WDES are the operational embodiment of systemic inclusion measurement — Critically Evaluating their assumptions (whether workforce band parity is the correct proxy for inclusion, whether WRES metrics capture lived experience of inclusion) strengthens the analysis significantly.

## Unconscious Bias — Critically Evaluate

Unconscious bias training is the most widely deployed organisational intervention for improving EDI outcomes in UK organisations. At Level 6, Critically Evaluating unconscious bias training requires engaging with the peer-reviewed evidence on its effectiveness.

**Critically Evaluate — evidence on effectiveness**: Forscher et al. (2019, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 117(4)) conducted a meta-analysis of 492 studies on implicit bias and found that: (1) implicit bias is measurable; (2) implicit bias can be reduced through training; (3) reduced implicit bias does not reliably translate into reduced biased behaviour. The gap between attitude change (which training can produce) and behavioural change (which training rarely sustains) is the critical limitation. At Level 6, citing Forscher et al. (2019) to Critically Evaluate the effectiveness assumption underlying unconscious bias training demonstrates engagement with the peer-reviewed evidence at Distinction standard.

**Alternative structural interventions**: Bohnet (2016, *What Works: Gender Equality by Design*, Harvard University Press) argues that structural redesign — changing the conditions under which decisions are made rather than trying to change individual decision-makers' attitudes — produces more reliable EDI outcomes than unconscious bias training. Structured interview processes (standardised questions, pre-agreed scoring criteria, diverse interview panels) reduce the influence of unconscious bias on recruitment outcomes without relying on individual bias reduction. At Level 6, the defensible position: structural redesign and process standardisation are more evidence-based EDI interventions than unconscious bias training alone.

## Pass / Merit / Distinction

**Pass:** Equality Act 2010 applied at senior management level. Intersectionality introduced. Systemic inclusion framework applied. Unconscious bias addressed. Peer-reviewed sources included.

**Merit:** Equality Act's single-axis and structural gap limitations Critically Evaluated. Crenshaw's intersectionality framework applied with specific examples. Diversity-without-inclusion evidence engaged. Forscher et al. (2019) meta-analysis on unconscious bias effectiveness cited.

**Distinction — worked example:** "Critically Evaluating the Trust's EDI strategy reveals a structural dependence on legal compliance and unconscious bias training that the peer-reviewed evidence identifies as insufficient for producing the inclusion outcomes the strategy targets. Forscher et al.'s (2019) meta-analysis — finding that unconscious bias reduction does not reliably translate into reduced biased behaviour — challenges the foundational assumption of the training programme that has received £220,000 of investment over the past three years. The WRES data for the same period shows no statistically significant improvement in representation of Black, Asian and minority ethnic staff at Band 7 and above, which is consistent with Forscher et al.'s finding. Crenshaw's intersectionality framework adds diagnostic precision: the WRES analysis treats race as a single-axis characteristic, producing data that masks the distinct experiences of South Asian, Black African, and Black Caribbean women, whose career progression barriers are not identical and whose combined race-sex intersection places them at the most acute disadvantage in the Trust's promotion pipeline. The defensible recommendation applies Bohnet's (2016) structural redesign approach: replacing the current discretionary promotion conversation process (which gives the highest-scoring implicit bias conditions — unstructured, relationship-dependent, senior panel without diversity criteria) with a structured, criteria-led process with a diverse assessment centre panel and transparent scoring. This structural change addresses the mechanism producing WRES underperformance, rather than the individual attitudes of assessors."

## Common Questions About CMI Unit 613

**How is Level 6 Unit 613 different from Level 4 Unit 408?**
Unit 408 at Level 4 applies the Equality Act at Analyse and Evaluate depth — analysing what the law requires in management practice, examining how direct and indirect discrimination arise, and evaluating management's role in preventing them. Unit 613 at Level 6 Critically Evaluates the Equality Act as a framework — examining what the law cannot achieve (structural inequality, intersectional discrimination, cultural exclusion) — and evaluates systemic inclusion frameworks that go beyond legal compliance. The Level 6 response engages with intersectionality theory (Crenshaw), the evidence on diversity intervention effectiveness (Forscher et al.), and structural redesign approaches (Bohnet) that are not addressed at Level 4.

**What is intersectionality and how do I apply it at Level 6?**
Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) is the theoretical framework for understanding how multiple systems of disadvantage — race, sex, class, disability, sexuality, age — intersect and compound rather than adding independently. A Black disabled woman does not experience three separate forms of disadvantage; she experiences the intersection of all three in ways that cannot be understood by analysing each characteristic separately. At Level 6, applying intersectionality means: (1) identifying the specific intersecting characteristics relevant to your organisational context; (2) examining how your organisation's EDI data and policies treat these characteristics separately (the Equality Act's single-axis limitation); and (3) evaluating what your EDI strategy misses by failing to analyse intersectional experience. For NHS students: analysing WRES data by ethnic category without disaggregating by gender misses the intersectional experience of Black women in the NHS workforce.

**What is the Forscher et al. (2019) finding and why does it matter for Unit 613?**
Forscher et al. (2019, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 117(4)) conducted a meta-analysis of 492 studies examining the relationship between implicit bias and explicit behaviour. They found that: interventions can reduce implicit bias (Implicit Association Test scores); but reduced implicit bias does not reliably predict reduced biased behaviour. The attitude-behaviour gap means that unconscious bias training can produce measurable attitude change without producing the behavioural change that EDI strategy requires. This matters at Level 6 because unconscious bias training is the most commonly deployed EDI intervention in UK organisations — and the evidence suggests it is being deployed as if attitude change produces behaviour change, which the empirical evidence does not support.

**What is the WRES and how does it relate to CMI Level 6 EDI analysis?**
The Workforce Race Equality Standard (WRES) is an NHS-mandated framework requiring all NHS organisations to report annually on nine metrics measuring the experience and representation of Black, Asian and minority ethnic staff relative to white staff. The WDES (Workforce Disability Equality Standard) provides equivalent metrics for disabled staff. At Level 6, WRES and WDES are the operational systemic EDI measurement frameworks for NHS students — they go beyond Equality Act compliance to measure structural inequality at workforce level. Critically Evaluating WRES at Level 6 means examining what it measures and what it misses: WRES measures representation at band level (does the proportion of BAME staff at each band reflect their overall workforce proportion?) but does not measure lived experience of inclusion, intersectional barriers, or the quality of development opportunities available.

**Can I write Unit 613 without reference to the NHS context?**
Yes. Unit 613 applies to any organisational context — commercial, public sector, third sector, professional services. The core frameworks (Equality Act, intersectionality, unconscious bias evidence, structural redesign) are sector-agnostic. NHS-specific references (WRES, WDES, NHS People Plan) are optional additions for NHS students that add contextual specificity. If you work in a commercial or third-sector organisation, use your organisation's EDI data, policies, and structural conditions as the basis for the systemic inclusion analysis. The Critically Evaluate analysis of the Equality Act's structural limitations, intersectionality's explanatory power, and the evidence on intervention effectiveness applies regardless of sector.
