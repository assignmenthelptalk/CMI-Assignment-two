## What CMI Unit 402 Covers

Unit 402 addresses self-management and personal development as management competencies. The learning outcomes require you to analyse your own management strengths and development needs, apply a learning framework to plan and reflect on professional development, and evaluate a personal development approach. The first-person reflective element is deliberate, this unit is not about describing frameworks in the abstract but applying them to your own management experience and practice.

## Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle — Analyse Depth

David Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle (1984, *Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development*, Prentice Hall) provides the theoretical model for understanding how managers learn from experience. The cycle has four stages: **Concrete Experience** (having an experience, doing or encountering something); **Reflective Observation** (reviewing and reflecting on the experience, what happened, how did it feel?); **Abstract Conceptualisation** (drawing conclusions and developing a theory or principle from the reflection, what does this mean in general?); and **Active Experimentation** (planning and trying out new approaches based on the conclusions, what will I do differently?).

At Level 4 Analyse depth, the Kolb analysis goes beyond describing the four stages. It examines what happens when a manager's learning process gets stuck at one stage:
- A manager who accumulates concrete experiences but never moves to reflection (skips Reflective Observation) repeats the same mistakes without understanding why.
- A manager who reflects extensively but never moves to conceptualisation (stays in Reflective Observation) generates self-awareness without the generalisable principles needed to transfer learning to new situations.
- A manager who theorises (Abstract Conceptualisation) without then experimenting (Active Experimentation) develops intellectual understanding without behavioural change.
- A manager who experiments without prior reflection and conceptualisation (jumps from experience directly to Active Experimentation) changes behaviours without understanding whether the change is grounded in evidence.

At Evaluate depth: identify at which stage your own learning cycle most commonly stalls, evaluate the consequences for your professional development, and form a conclusion about what specific practices would complete the cycle more effectively. A manager who identifies that they consistently skip Reflective Observation because of time pressure can evaluate whether the consequence (repeated experience without learning) justifies investing time in structured reflection, and conclude with a specific reflection practice they will adopt.

## Honey and Mumford's Learning Styles

Honey and Mumford (1982) extended Kolb's cycle into four learning style preferences, Activist, Reflector, Theorist, Pragmatist, that correspond to the stage of the cycle the individual most naturally gravitates toward. At Level 4 Analyse depth: analyse which learning style preference dominates your own approach and what this means for how you engage with professional development opportunities. An Activist manager who favours concrete experience and active experimentation may underinvest in reflective practice and theoretical frameworks, which creates a systematic gap in the Reflective Observation and Abstract Conceptualisation stages that reduces the quality of learning from experience.

## The Johari Window — Analyse and Evaluate Depth

Joseph Luft and Harry Ingham's Johari Window (1955) is a model of self-awareness and interpersonal effectiveness that maps information about the self across four quadrants: **Open** (known to self, known to others, behaviour, skills, attitudes that are visible and acknowledged); **Hidden** (known to self, not known to others, aspects the individual chooses not to share); **Blind** (not known to self, known to others, how others perceive the individual in ways they cannot directly observe); and **Unknown** (not known to self, not known to others, undiscovered potential and unconscious aspects).

At Level 4 Analyse depth: analyse how the size and content of each quadrant affects management effectiveness. The Blind quadrant is analytically the most significant for management development, it represents the gap between the manager's self-perception and how they are actually experienced by team members, peers, and line managers. A manager whose Blind quadrant includes defensive reactions to criticism, communication styles that produce anxiety rather than clarity, or leadership behaviours that undermine the inclusion they believe they are creating will continue to generate those consequences despite genuine commitment to improvement.

The mechanism for reducing the Blind quadrant is feedback, seeking specific, behavioural feedback from trusted others, particularly from team members who directly experience the manager's behaviour. The Hidden quadrant can be reduced through selective self-disclosure, choosing to share relevant personal context that helps others understand the manager's perspective or constraints. As both Blind and Hidden quadrants reduce, the Open quadrant grows, increasing mutual understanding and interpersonal effectiveness.

At Evaluate depth: evaluate the current size and significance of your own Blind quadrant, based on feedback received, 360-degree assessments, or discrepancies between intended and experienced impact, and form a conclusion about the most valuable development focus. If the most significant Blind quadrant content involves how the manager is experienced under pressure (short communication, visible impatience, reduced consultation), the development priority is self-regulation under stress, connecting back to Goleman's EI framework in Unit 401.

## Personal Development Planning

A personal development plan (PDP) at Level 4 connects the analysis of strengths and development needs (Johari Window diagnosis) to planned development activities (Kolb's learning cycle operationalised). Key components at Analyse and Evaluate depth: what is the development need (specific and behaviourally defined)? What is the evidence base for this need (performance data, feedback, self-assessment)? What development activity will address it, and why (rationale for the chosen approach rather than alternatives)? What will success look like, and by when? How will the development be reviewed?

At Evaluate depth: evaluate which development approach is most likely to produce lasting behavioural change for the specific need identified. For a development need rooted in self-awareness deficit (large Blind quadrant), the most effective approach is regular structured feedback rather than a training course, because the learning need is experiential and relational rather than cognitive.

## Pass / Merit / Distinction

**Pass:** Kolb's stages are applied to a personal development experience. Johari Window quadrants are described with self-referential content. A development plan is outlined. Workplace examples included.

**Merit:** The stage at which the personal learning cycle stalls is identified and analysed with evidence. The Blind quadrant is engaged with specifically, not just defined abstractly. The PDP connects development activities to identified needs with reasoning.

**Distinction, worked example:** "Applying Kolb's cycle to my experience of a challenging performance conversation with a senior team member, I can identify that I moved from Concrete Experience directly to Active Experimentation without completing the Reflective Observation stage, I attempted a different approach in the next conversation without understanding what had caused the breakdown in the first. The Johari Window analysis adds context: feedback from my line manager (via 360-degree review) identified that my communication under pressure was experienced as dismissive, a Blind quadrant insight that I had not accessed from my own self-perception. The connection between the two frameworks reveals a pattern: I am an Activist learner (Honey and Mumford) who skips reflection, and the consequence is that Blind quadrant content, my dismissive communication under pressure, persists because I am not completing the reflective stage of the learning cycle that would allow me to receive and process feedback about its impact. The development priority is not a different approach to performance conversations but a structured reflection practice after difficult conversations, incorporating specific feedback-seeking from the team member, that would shrink the Blind quadrant and complete the learning cycle."

## Common Questions About CMI Unit 402

**How personal does Unit 402 need to be, can I use hypothetical examples?**
Unit 402 specifically requires self-referential application, the brief asks you to apply frameworks to your own management experience and professional development. Using entirely hypothetical examples does not meet the assessment criteria. If confidentiality is a concern, describe your experiences with sufficient anonymisation (refer to "a recent performance conversation" rather than naming individuals) while maintaining the personal, reflective quality the LOs require.

**What is the Johari Window's Blind quadrant and how do I identify what is in mine?**
The Blind quadrant contains information about you that others can observe but you cannot see in yourself. The primary access routes are: formal feedback mechanisms (360-degree assessment, appraisal feedback, line manager input); informal feedback from trusted colleagues; and observations of discrepancy between intended impact and actual impact (when a conversation you intended to be supportive produced defensiveness, when communication you intended to be clear produced confusion). At Level 4, the analytical contribution is naming specific Blind quadrant content, not just acknowledging that a Blind quadrant exists, and connecting it to development priority.

**Can I use Honey and Mumford instead of Kolb for the learning theory LO?**
Honey and Mumford (1982) is directly derived from Kolb (1984), and either is appropriate. Most Unit 402 briefs reference a learning cycle or learning styles framework, using both Kolb (for the developmental process) and Honey & Mumford (for the individual style preference) provides fuller coverage than using only one. If your brief specifies one framework, use that one. If it does not specify, using both demonstrates theoretical depth without adding excessive length.

**What is the difference between Unit 402 and Unit 513 (Reflective Practice) at Level 5?**
Unit 402 at Level 4 applies self-development frameworks (Kolb, Johari Window) to personal management effectiveness at Analyse and Evaluate depth. Unit 513 at Level 5 is specifically a reflective account, it uses Gibbs' or Kolb's reflective models to structure a first-person reflective account of professional practice at Evaluate depth, submitted in a specific reflective format (not essay format). The key difference: Unit 402 applies learning frameworks to personal development; Unit 513 evaluates the reflective process itself as a professional practice tool.

**How long should the personal development plan section be in Unit 402?**
The PDP section is typically one of two to three sections in the Unit 402 essay, and need not be the longest. It should be specific enough to demonstrate that the development needs have been accurately identified (connected to Johari Window analysis and Kolb cycle stage diagnosis), that the development activities are appropriate for the needs (not generic), and that evaluation criteria and timescales are in place. Approximately 400–500 words for the PDP section is typical, more detailed plans can be included as a brief table in an appendix if word count is tight.
