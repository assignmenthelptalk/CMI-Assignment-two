## Reflective Practice Frameworks at Critically Analyse Depth

### Kolb (1984) — The Experiential Learning Cycle

David Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle, published in *Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development* (Kolb, 1984), proposes that learning occurs through a four-stage cycle:

**Concrete Experience** — a specific event or situation the learner encounters directly.
**Reflective Observation** — reviewing and reflecting on the experience.
**Abstract Conceptualisation** — drawing generalisable conclusions and forming new theories.
**Active Experimentation** — testing the new understanding in practice, generating new concrete experiences.

At Critically Analyse depth for Unit 701, the student does not apply Kolb's cycle to a development experience — they engage with what Kolb (1984) claimed and what the subsequent evidence supports. The critical engagement: Kolb's model is descriptive rather than prescriptive. It describes how learning *can* occur through experience but does not specify the conditions under which learners progress from one stage to the next, why learners may have preferences for certain stages over others, or how the cycle operates in organisational contexts where time and safety for reflection are constrained.

Kolb's (1984) associated learning style inventory — which maps learners to dominant stages (Diverger, Assimilator, Converger, Accommodator) — has been criticised for low test-retest reliability and for suggesting fixed learning preferences rather than adaptive learning flexibility. For strategic leaders, who must be capable of learning across all four stages, the implication of stable style preferences is theoretically problematic.

### Argyris and Schön (1978) — Single-Loop and Double-Loop Learning

Chris Argyris and Donald Schön's *Organizational Learning: A Theory of Action Perspective* (1978) introduces a distinction that is central to Critically Analyse engagement in Unit 701:

**Single-loop learning** — detecting and correcting errors within the existing governing variables (norms, values, strategies) of an organisation or individual. The governing variables themselves are not questioned. A manager who identifies that a team performance problem is caused by poor briefing and improves their briefing practice is engaging in single-loop learning.

**Double-loop learning** — questioning the governing variables themselves — the assumptions, values, and frameworks that define what counts as a problem and what constitutes a solution. A manager who asks whether the performance management framework that generates the briefing requirements is producing the right outcomes is engaging in double-loop learning.

For strategic leaders, double-loop learning is the appropriate development standard. Strategic leadership requires the capacity to question the governing assumptions of the organisation — the strategy, the business model, the culture — not just to correct execution errors within them. Kolb's (1984) cycle, Argyris and Schön (1978) argue, tends toward single-loop learning: the Abstract Conceptualisation stage produces new theories-in-use, but those theories remain within the learner's existing frame of reference unless double-loop questioning is deliberately initiated.

The Critically Analyse move for Unit 701: what does the Argyris and Schön (1978) double-loop distinction imply for how strategic leaders should design their personal development? If most development frameworks — including Kolb and Gibbs — operate at single-loop depth, what would a genuinely double-loop development practice look like at strategic leadership level?

### Gibbs (1988) — The Reflective Cycle

Graham Gibbs' Reflective Cycle (*Learning by Doing: A Guide to Teaching and Learning Methods*, 1988) provides six stages:

**Description** → **Feelings** → **Evaluation** → **Analysis** → **Conclusion** → **Action Plan**

Gibbs' model is more structured than Kolb's and is explicitly designed to support practitioners in extracting transferable learning from specific experiences. It is widely used in professional development contexts — including nursing, teaching, and management — because the staged structure guides practitioners through reflection rather than assuming reflective capacity is self-generating.

At Critically Analyse depth: Gibbs' model, like Kolb's, operates primarily at single-loop depth — the Action Plan stage produces changed practice within the existing framework of what good practice looks like, rather than questioning the framework. For strategic leaders, who need development frameworks that support the questioning of strategic assumptions, both Kolb and Gibbs require supplementation with double-loop mechanisms.

## Goleman's Emotional Intelligence at Strategic Leadership Level

Daniel Goleman's Emotional Intelligence framework (*Emotional Intelligence*, 1995; *Working with Emotional Intelligence*, 1998) identifies five domains relevant to strategic leader development:

**Self-Awareness** — the ability to recognise one's own emotions, strengths, weaknesses, and their impact on others.
**Self-Regulation** — the ability to manage disruptive emotions and impulses; consistency of values under pressure.
**Motivation** — intrinsic drive to achieve, optimism in the face of setbacks, commitment to goals beyond external reward.
**Empathy** — understanding the emotional makeup of others; skill in responding to emotional cues.
**Social Skills** — proficiency in managing relationships, building networks, finding common ground, and building rapport.

At Critically Analyse depth for Unit 701, the student engages with the academic critique of Goleman's framework. Goleman's popular EI model — particularly as presented in the 1995 and 1998 books — has been criticised by researchers including Locke (2005) for conceptual overreach: the construct has been expanded to include so many traits (personality, motivation, social skill) that it becomes too broad to measure or falsify distinctly. The original ability-based EI model developed by Salovey and Mayer (1990) — which defines EI as the ability to perceive, use, understand, and manage emotions — is narrower, more empirically grounded, and theoretically distinct from personality traits.

For Unit 701, the Critically Analyse requirement is: engage with this debate. Does Goleman's (1995, 1998) broad EI framework or Salovey and Mayer's (1990) ability-based model provide a more theoretically sound basis for strategic leader development? What are the implications of this distinction for how a senior leader approaches self-awareness and self-regulation development in practice?

Single-loop learning produces improved execution; double-loop learning produces changed assumptions. For strategic leaders, double-loop learning is the appropriate development standard because strategic leadership requires the capacity to question the strategic frameworks, business models, and cultural assumptions of the organisation — not only to improve execution within them. Both Kolb's and Gibbs' cycles tend toward single-loop depth; Unit 701 requires the student to engage with this limitation.
