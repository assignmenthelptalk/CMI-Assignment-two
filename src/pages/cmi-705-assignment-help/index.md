## Barney (1991) — The Resource-Based View and VRIO Framework

Jay Barney's 'Firm resources and sustained competitive advantage' (*Journal of Management*, 17(1), pp. 99–120, 1991) is the foundational text of the Resource-Based View of competitive advantage. Barney (1991) argues that competitive advantage comes not from industry structure or market positioning (Porter's framework) but from firm-specific resources that are heterogeneous across firms and imperfectly mobile between them.

### The VRIO Framework at Critically Analyse Depth

Barney (1991) proposes four criteria for resources to generate sustained competitive advantage:

**Valuable:** The resource enables the firm to exploit opportunities or neutralise threats in its competitive environment. A resource that cannot be connected to a strategic opportunity or threat mitigation is not a source of competitive advantage regardless of its other characteristics.

**Rare:** The resource is controlled by few, if any, current or potential competitors. A resource that is valuable but widely available across competitors may produce competitive parity, necessary for survival, but not competitive advantage.

**Inimitable:** The resource is difficult or costly for competitors to imitate. Barney (1991) identifies three sources of inimitability:

- **Path dependency**: the resource has accumulated over time through unique historical experiences that cannot be replicated by competitors attempting to acquire the resource today. An organisational culture built over decades of specific leadership decisions cannot be purchased or rapidly reproduced.

- **Causal ambiguity**: the link between the resource and the competitive advantage it produces is not fully understood, even by the firm itself. Competitors cannot imitate what they cannot clearly identify. Tacit knowledge, complex social relationships, and embedded organisational routines generate causal ambiguity.

- **Social complexity**: the resource involves complex social phenomena, interpersonal relationships, trust, organisational culture, leadership networks, that cannot be systematically managed or replicated even when they are fully understood. A competitor may identify that a firm's competitive advantage derives from its senior leadership team's mutual trust and complementary capabilities, but cannot replicate those relationships by hiring the individuals.

**Organised:** The firm has the organisational structures, processes, and systems to capture value from the resource. A resource that is Valuable, Rare, and Inimitable but not captured by the organisation's strategic architecture produces only potential rather than actual competitive advantage.

### The Critically Analyse Engagement with Barney (1991)

At Critically Analyse depth, the student engages with what Barney (1991) assumed and where those assumptions create theoretical limitations.

**The static environment assumption**: Barney's (1991) VRIO framework analyses resource positions at a point in time. It predicts which resources will sustain competitive advantage given a stable competitive environment. In dynamic environments, characterised by rapid technological change, shifting demand, and regulatory disruption, a resource bundle that is VRIO-positive today may become obsolete tomorrow without warning. The framework is ill-equipped to account for how resources must be reconfigured over time.

**The tautology risk**: critics of RBV argue that the framework is potentially circular, resources that produce sustained competitive advantage are defined by their VRIO characteristics, but whether a resource is truly inimitable or truly valuable is only visible through the competitive advantage it produces. Identifying VRIO-positive resources prospectively rather than retrospectively is theoretically problematic.

**The unit of analysis**: Barney (1991) focuses on resource stocks, what the firm has. This raises the question of how firms acquire, develop, and renew VRIO-positive resources over time, which the RBV does not fully address, the gap that Teece et al. (1997) directly respond to.

## Teece, Pisano and Shuen (1997) — Dynamic Capabilities

David Teece, Gary Pisano, and Amy Shuen's 'Dynamic capabilities and strategic management' (*Strategic Management Journal*, 18(7), pp. 509–533, 1997) responds directly to the limitations of Barney's (1991) static RBV. Teece et al. (1997, p. 516) define dynamic capabilities as "the firm's ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competences to address rapidly changing environments."

### Three Dynamic Capability Processes

**Sensing:** The ability to identify, develop, co-develop, and assess technological opportunities and threats, customer evolution, and market shifts. Sensing is not passive scanning, it requires organisational investment in research, customer intelligence, and environmental monitoring.

**Seizing:** The ability to mobilise resources, including through investment decisions, organisational design, and partner selection, to address sensed opportunities. Seizing requires strategic leaders to commit to new resource configurations before the full outcome is certain.

**Reconfiguring:** The ability to continuously renew, combine, protect, and reconfigure organisational assets and structures. In Schumpeterian competition, characterised by innovation-driven cycles of creative destruction, the ability to reconfigure is itself the source of sustained advantage because the reconfiguration capability, not any specific resource configuration, is what persists.

### The Dynamic Capabilities vs RBV Debate — The Critically Analyse Content

The central theoretical tension for Unit 705: Barney (1991) argues that sustained competitive advantage comes from WHAT a firm has, resources that are VRIO-positive. Teece et al. (1997) argue that in dynamic environments, sustained advantage comes from HOW a firm adapts what it has, the process capability to sense, seize, and reconfigure.

The Critically Analyse engagement requires the student to take a position: under what conditions does Barney's (1991) VRIO framework better explain strategic leadership's contribution to competitive advantage, and under what conditions does Teece et al.'s (1997) Dynamic Capabilities framework better explain it?

One theoretically grounded position: in stable or slowly evolving competitive environments (some public sector contexts, regulated industries with slow technological change), VRIO-positive resource positions can sustain advantage over extended periods, Barney (1991) has stronger explanatory power. In dynamic environments (technology-intensive sectors, rapidly changing healthcare delivery models, digital disruption contexts), the ability to sense and reconfigure matters more than the specific resource configuration at any point, Teece et al. (1997) has stronger explanatory power. A strategic leader's primary task differs accordingly: in stable environments, protecting and leveraging VRIO resources; in dynamic environments, building and exercising the sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring capabilities.

This is an original synthesis, it does not merely repeat what either primary source says but derives a theoretically grounded position from engaging with both.

## Ulrich — Strategic Leadership as Organisational Capability

Ulrich and Smallwood's *Leadership Brand* (2007) provides a third theoretical lens for Unit 705's LO3. Ulrich and Smallwood argue that sustained competitive advantage requires not individual strategic leaders with VRIO characteristics but an organisational leadership capability, consistent, replicable leadership behaviour across the organisation that connects to customer and investor value.

The connection to Barney (1991): an organisation's leadership brand is an inimitable resource, it develops through path-dependent accumulation of leadership practices, involves causal ambiguity (competitors cannot identify precisely which leadership behaviours produce the organisational outcomes), and is socially complex (embedded in relationships, culture, and collective practice). The leadership brand satisfies all four VRIO criteria.

The connection to Teece et al. (1997): the capacity to develop and renew the leadership brand over time, sensing leadership capability requirements as strategy changes, seizing development opportunities, reconfiguring the leadership architecture in response to strategic environment shifts, is itself a dynamic capability.

**What is VRIO and how is it used in CMI Unit 705?**

Barney (1991) proposed that resources producing sustained competitive advantage must be Valuable (enabling exploitation of opportunities or neutralisation of threats), Rare (controlled by few competitors), Inimitable (costly to replicate, due to path dependency, causal ambiguity, or social complexity), and Organised (captured by the firm's systems and structures). In Unit 705, VRIO is not applied to a firm's product portfolio, it is applied to the strategic leadership capability itself: is the organisation's strategic leadership a VRIO-positive resource? The Critically Analyse engagement requires naming the limitations of the framework, not just applying it.

**What is the difference between the RBV and Dynamic Capabilities in CMI Unit 705?**

Barney's (1991) Resource-Based View treats competitive advantage as a function of the resource stocks a firm possesses, what the firm has at a given point in time. Teece, Pisano and Shuen's (1997) Dynamic Capabilities framework treats competitive advantage in dynamic environments as a function of the process capabilities a firm has to continuously sense, seize, and reconfigure its resource base. The RBV is static, it analyses resource positions. Dynamic Capabilities is processual, it analyses the firm's ability to change resource positions in response to environmental shifts. The debate for Unit 705 is: which better explains how strategic leaders create and sustain competitive advantage?
